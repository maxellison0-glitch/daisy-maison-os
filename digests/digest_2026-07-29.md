## 📚 Lesson of the Day (confidential - not for Daryl)

**Regression to the mean**

This builds on the sample-size lesson from 9 Jul, which explained that a single day's reading on a small number of events is mostly noise and needs a real sample before you trust it. Today's lesson takes that same caution and points it at something the digest itself has been doing every morning without saying so out loud: predicting a day's result from "the last few times this weekday happened."

Here is the idea in plain English. **Regression to the mean** is the tendency for an unusually good (or unusually bad) run of results to be followed by a result that drifts back toward the *true, long-run* average - not because anything changed, but simply because the earlier run was, at least in part, luck. It happens anywhere small samples meet random variation: a golfer's hot week, a shop's best month, or - relevant here - three Tuesdays in a row that all happened to land green. A **sample** is just the set of actual observations you have (here, individual Tuesdays); when a sample is small, its average is itself a noisy, unreliable estimate of the "real" underlying average, even though it feels solid because it's the only evidence you've got. A **baseline** or **prediction interval**, as this digest builds one every morning from "the last few of this weekday," is only as trustworthy as the sample size behind it - three data points is not enough to separate a genuine weekly pattern from a lucky streak.

Here's why it happens, mechanically. Every day's result is a mix of a real underlying tendency (some weekdays probably do convert a bit better than others, for structural reasons - people browsing gifts differently on different days) plus a large dose of pure noise (which specific auctions won, which specific browsers happened to be in a buying mood, whether a big personalised order landed that day). With only three or four observations of a given weekday, that noise hasn't had a chance to average out - so a short run of green Tuesdays can just as easily be "the real Tuesday pattern plus three lucky rolls" as "three unlucky rolls that happened to look similar." From the outside, this shows up as a day that "should" have been strong on paper coming in far below the range that paper implied - and it can feel like something broke in the account, when the more honest explanation is that the range itself was built on too little data and was never as tight as it looked.

Worked example, using yesterday's real numbers. The last three Tuesdays - 7, 14 and 21 Jul - ran ROAS 3.66x, 3.41x and 3.41x on spend of GBP 690.82, GBP 634.44 and GBP 584.24. Using that band against yesterday's GBP 715.47 spend: 715.47 × 3.41 = GBP 2,439.75 at the low end, 715.47 × 3.66 = GBP 2,618.62 at the high end - a predicted range of roughly GBP 2,440-2,619. Actual total sales were GBP 1,429.91. That's (2,439.75 - 1,429.91) ÷ 2,439.75 = 41.4% below even the low end of the range - not a near-miss, a collapse of the entire predicted band. Notice this happened on the *highest*-spend Tuesday of the four, which rules out "we just spent less" as the explanation; the true Tuesday average, it turns out, was never as tightly 3.41x-3.66x as three data points made it look, and yesterday's PMAX overspend (section 9) gave the account every reason to land on the low side of a much wider real range.

**Ask Daryl this:** "How many weeks of a given weekday do you personally want to see before you trust a day-of-week ROAS pattern in this account, and can you pull the ROAS for every Tuesday we've run this year, not just the last three?" A good answer names a concrete number (most experienced media buyers want 6-8+ observations before calling a weekday pattern real) and can actually produce the fuller history on request. An evasive answer waves it off with "Tuesdays are just strong" or has no fuller data to hand - a sign the account isn't being read with real statistical care, just pattern-matched from whatever the last few weeks happened to show.

**You should now be able to:** treat any "based on the last few [weekday]s" prediction in this digest as a rough steer rather than a firm target, and ask for the fuller history before accepting that a weekday pattern is real rather than a short lucky or unlucky run.

## Lesson of the Day

### ROAS vs Actual Profit Margin

**Callback:** In "Contribution after ad spend" (21 Jul), you subtracted ad spend from total sales to see what's left. Today goes one layer deeper — that "what's left" number still has to cover every other cost before a penny reaches your pocket.

**What it is:** ROAS (Return on Ad Spend) measures how many pounds of *revenue* each pound of advertising bought. Your 2.41x yesterday means every £1 of ad spend brought back £2.41 in total sales. But revenue is not profit. ROAS ignores every cost between the sale and your bank account. Profit margin is the percentage of revenue that remains after ALL costs are deducted. **Gross margin** covers just product costs (materials, printing, manufacturing — the money you spend making the thing). **Net margin** covers everything: product costs plus ad spend plus platform fees plus payment processing plus shipping plus packaging plus overhead. ROAS measures the top line only. Margin measures the bottom line. A business can have brilliant ROAS and still lose money.

**Why it matters — the mechanism:** A 3x ROAS looks healthy. But take a £17 street sign sold at 3x ROAS. The ad cost attributable to that sale is £5.67 (£17 ÷ 3). Now stack on the real costs:

- Product cost (say 35% of sale price): £5.95
- Shopify subscription + transaction fees (~3%): £0.51
- Payment processing (Stripe, ~2.5%): £0.43
- Shipping: ~£3.50
- Packaging: ~£1.50
- **Total costs: £17.56**
- **Profit: −£0.56**

At 3x ROAS, a standalone £17 sign *loses money*. The ROAS number looked green. The margin is red.

This is why AOV is a margin lever, not just a vanity metric. When a customer adds mounting strips (£1.99) and an easel (£4.95), the product cost on those add-ons is tiny — maybe £0.50 combined — and they ship in the same package. No extra shipping, no extra payment processing on the base amount, trivial packaging cost. Those add-ons are almost pure margin. A £24 order at 3x ROAS is profitable where a £17 order is not, because the fixed costs (shipping, packaging, Stripe's flat per-transaction fee) are spread across more revenue.

**Worked example — Monday 21 September:**

Yesterday: £1,210.91 total sales, £502.98 ad spend, 43 orders, AOV £28.16.

ROAS = £1,210.91 ÷ £502.98 = **2.41x**

Revenue after ad spend: £1,210.91 − £502.98 = £707.93 (58.5% of revenue survives)

Now stack the other costs (using directional Daisy Maison estimates):
- Product costs (~35% of gross £981.77): ~£343.62
- Shopify fees (~3% of total): ~£36.33
- Payment processing (~2.5% of total): ~£30.27
- Shipping (~£3.50 × 43 orders): ~£150.50
- Packaging (~£1.50 × 43 orders): ~£64.50
- **Total non-ad costs: ~£625.22**

Estimated actual profit: £1,210.91 − £502.98 − £625.22 = **£82.71** → **6.8% net margin**

At 3x ROAS on the same cost base, ad spend would have been £403.64 instead of £502.98, saving £99.34 — pushing profit to ~£182 and margin to ~15%. The gap between 2.41x and 3x is not just a number on a dashboard. It is the difference between covering overheads and having room to invest.

These costs are directional estimates — you need real COGS from your accounts to get actual margin. But the structure of the calculation holds, and it shows why add-on attach rates are a *margin* lever, not just a revenue number.

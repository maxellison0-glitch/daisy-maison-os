## 📚 Lesson of the Day

### Revenue Per Session — The Number That Tells You Whether Traffic Is Worth Buying

**Builds on:** Contribution after ad spend (21 Jul) showed you how much money is left after subtracting ad costs from total sales. Today's concept isolates that question per visitor, giving you a single number that diagnoses whether a soft day is a traffic-quality problem or a traffic-volume problem — before you reach for the budget lever.

Revenue Per Session (RPS) is total sales divided by sessions. Yesterday: £1,437 ÷ 875 = **£1.64 per session**. Every person who visited the store was worth £1.64 on average — including the 96% who didn't buy anything. It's a small number, but it's the number the entire business runs on, because if it's higher than what you paid to get that person there, you're making money, and if it's lower, you're losing it.

RPS is powerful because it combines two things you already track — conversion rate (what proportion of visitors buy) and AOV (how much they spend when they do) — into a single metric that answers the only question that actually matters before a spend decision: "Is the next session I buy going to be profitable?"

**The mechanism.** When revenue drops on a given day, exactly three things could have moved: fewer sessions (less traffic — the store is fine, fewer people showed up), lower conversion rate (same traffic, fewer buyers — something about the audience or the store shifted), or lower AOV (same buyers, spending less — product mix or discounting changed). RPS captures all three in one number. If sessions drop but RPS stays constant, the store is healthy and the fix is "buy more traffic." If sessions are constant but RPS drops, the traffic is poor or the site is broken, and spending more would actually make things worse — each new session generates less revenue than it costs.

This is why blanket decisions like "increase budget" or "cut budget" are both sometimes right and sometimes catastrophic. The question isn't "should we spend more?" It's "what is the next session worth?"

**The failure mode you can now spot.** RPS falling while spend rises means you're paying more for worse traffic. That's exactly what happened on 28 Jul with the PMAX overspend: more budget pushed the algorithm into less-interested audiences, each additional session cost the same but generated less revenue, and the day produced the worst ROAS of the tracked run. The reverse failure — healthy RPS with falling sessions — means the account is shrinking: traffic quality is fine but the ads aren't reaching enough people, and that's where careful scaling makes sense.

**Worked example — yesterday vs the 28 Jul overspend:**

| Metric | 28 Jul (overspend) | 31 Jul (yesterday) |
|---|---|---|
| Total sales | ~£1,430 | £1,437 |
| Sessions | ~1,028 (est.) | 875 |
| Ad spend | £715 | £573 |
| **RPS** | ~£1.39 | **£1.64** |
| Cost per session | ~£0.70 | £0.65 |

Nearly identical top-line revenue. But yesterday produced it from 153 fewer sessions at £142 less ad spend. The arithmetic shows exactly why the spend cut worked:

- **28 Jul:** At ~60% gross margin, £1.39 × 0.60 = £0.83 product margin per session. Minus £0.70 ad cost = **13p net per session**.
- **31 Jul:** £1.64 × 0.60 = £0.98 product margin per session. Minus £0.65 ad cost = **33p net per session**.

Same revenue. 2.5× more profit per session. The extra £142 in ad spend on 28 Jul bought sessions that generated roughly 13p each in profit; yesterday's sessions generated 33p. The budget cut didn't lose revenue — it shed unprofitable traffic.

**ASK DARYL THIS:** "What's our cost per session running at on PMAX versus TOF-WEDDING right now?" Yesterday, Google delivered 628 clicks at £361.64 (about £0.58/click) and Facebook delivered 574 clicks at £211.46 (about £0.37/click). But clicks aren't sessions — some clicks bounce before becoming sessions, and some sessions come from organic search. A good answer separates the platforms and links cost-per-session to what each platform's traffic actually produces in revenue. An evasive answer talks about total spend or CPC without connecting it to session quality.

**You should now be able to** divide any day's total sales by its session count and know instantly whether the problem is traffic quality (falling RPS on stable sessions), traffic volume (stable RPS on fewer sessions), or both — and which one the spend decision should target.

## 📚 Lesson of the Day

### Ad Set Budget Reallocation — Moving Money to Where It Converts

**Callback:** Yesterday's lesson explained how Facebook's pixel fires Purchase events and how the algorithm uses them to build a buyer profile. Today builds on that — once you know an ad set IS generating purchases, the next question is whether each purchase costs more or less than the ad set next to it, and what to do about the difference.

**What Cost Per Purchase (CPP) is:**

Cost Per Purchase is the amount you spend on ads to generate one completed sale. The formula: **CPP = ad set spend ÷ number of purchases attributed to that ad set**. It is the most important number at the ad set level because it directly answers: "How much did it cost me to get a customer?"

CPC (cost per click) tells you how much traffic costs. CTR (click-through rate) tells you how many people found the ad interesting. Neither tells you whether those people actually bought anything. Two ad sets can have completely different CPC and CTR but identical CPP — or, critically, one can have worse CPC and worse CTR but still deliver cheaper purchases, because its clicks are from people more likely to buy. **CPP is the final arbiter. Everything else is an intermediate metric.**

**Why Facebook doesn't always get this right:**

When you have two ad sets in the same campaign, you might assume Facebook automatically sends more money to the one generating cheaper purchases. Sometimes it does — but not always, and especially not in ABO (Ad Set Budget Optimization). Three failure modes:

1. **Audience size bias.** Facebook favours ad sets with larger audiences because there is more room to deliver impressions, even if those impressions convert less efficiently.
2. **Data inertia.** An ad set that has been running longer has more accumulated data. Facebook trusts it more, even if a newer ad set is outperforming it on CPP.
3. **ABO locks the allocation.** In Ad Set Budget Optimization — which is what TOF-WEDDING runs — **YOU set each ad set's budget, and Facebook does not redistribute between them**. If you set "Copy" at £150/day and "more" at £50/day, that is exactly what Facebook spends, regardless of which one converts better. This is the most common failure mode, and it is entirely the advertiser's fault, not Facebook's.

The distinction from CBO (Campaign Budget Optimization, taught on 13 Aug) is critical: in CBO, Facebook shifts budget automatically between ad sets to chase the cheapest event. In ABO, budget allocation is your decision. Nobody else is making it.

**Worked example — yesterday's Daisy data:**

TOF-WEDDING ran two ad sets on 31 August:

| Ad Set | Spend | Clicks | CPC | CTR | Purchases | CPP | FB Revenue |
|--------|-------|--------|-----|-----|-----------|-----|------------|
| Copy | £169.25 | 487 | £0.35 | 1.01% | 14 | £12.09 | £336.80 |
| more | £52.63 | 319 | £0.17 | 2.79% | 5 | £10.53 | £160.45 |

Look at CPC: "more" buys clicks at half the price (£0.17 vs £0.35). Its CTR is nearly 3× better (2.79% vs 1.01%). And its CPP — the number that actually matters — is lower: £10.53 vs £12.09. Each purchase from "more" costs £1.56 less.

But "Copy" received 76% of the campaign's spend (£169 of £222). Because this is ABO, that split is a deliberate budget setting — not Facebook choosing "Copy" because it is smarter, but because someone set "Copy's" budget higher and never revisited it.

Now do the maths on reallocation. If you moved £50/day from "Copy" to "more," keeping total campaign spend at £222:

- At "more's" current CPP of £10.53, that £50 buys **~4.7 additional purchases**
- At "Copy's" current CPP of £12.09, that £50 was buying **~4.1 purchases**
- Net gain: **~0.6 additional purchases per day at the same total spend**
- Over 30 days: **~18 additional purchases for free**

The gain looks modest on a single day, but compound it over a month and it is real money — roughly £500+ in additional FB-attributed revenue at zero extra cost. And if "more's" CPP advantage widens as it gets more data (more purchase events improve its targeting algorithm per yesterday's 50-event rule), the gap grows.

**The risk:** Moving budget too aggressively can push "more" past its audience sweet spot, raising its CPP. The rule of thumb: shift no more than 20–30% of the donor ad set's budget per change, then wait 3 days before moving again.

**Where to do this in Ads Manager:**

1. Open Ads Manager → click the TOF-WEDDING campaign name → you see the ad set list
2. Click the "more" ad set row → Edit → Budget & Schedule → change Daily Budget upward
3. Click the "Copy" ad set row → Edit → Budget & Schedule → reduce Daily Budget by the same amount
4. The change takes effect immediately. Facebook adjusts delivery within 30 minutes

Start moderate: move £30–50/day from "Copy" to "more." Watch CPP for 3 days. If "more's" CPP stays below "Copy's" after the increase, move another £30.

**TikTok note:** TikTok Ads Manager has identical ad group-level budget controls, but audiences fatigue in 3–5 days (vs 2–4 weeks on Facebook), so reallocation decisions happen more frequently.

**ASK DARYL THIS:** "What was the rationale for setting Copy's budget at roughly 3× the budget of 'more'? Is it targeting a different audience, or is it the legacy default from when Copy was the only ad set?" A good answer references a specific audience definition or a past performance reason. An evasive answer is "it's just how it was set up" — that means it has never been reviewed.

**You should now be able to:** Open Ads Manager, compare Cost Per Purchase between ad sets in the same campaign, and move budget toward the more efficient ad set using the 20–30% shift rule.

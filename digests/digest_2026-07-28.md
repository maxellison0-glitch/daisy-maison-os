## 📚 Lesson of the Day (confidential - not for Daryl)

**Diminishing returns on ad spend - and why cutting a budget doesn't "un-cut" it instantly**

This builds on the marginal-versus-blended-ROAS lesson from 6 Jul, which explained that the return on the *next* pound of spend can be very different from the account's average return. Today's lesson is the same idea looked at from the other direction: what actually happens, hour by hour, when you turn the dial down instead of up - and why the account doesn't obediently snap back to a better ROAS the moment you do.

Start with the auction. Every time someone searches or scrolls, Google and Facebook run a real-time auction to decide whose ad gets shown, ranking every advertiser by a mix of bid and predicted usefulness to that specific person. When a campaign has a modest daily budget, the algorithm can afford to be picky - it only enters the auctions where it's most confident of a cheap, high-quality result, so early spend buys the best available slice of the audience. As you raise the budget, the campaign has to keep spending once it's already won the good auctions, so it starts entering auctions for people it's less sure about - browsers, not buyers, or people further from a personalised gift purchase. That's "diminishing returns": each additional pound tends to buy a slightly worse customer than the pound before it, because the best customers get claimed first.

The mechanism matters just as much running in reverse. When you cut a budget, you might expect the account to simply retreat to the good auctions it started with and ROAS to bounce straight back up. It doesn't, for two reasons already met in this column. First, the "learning phase" (6 Jun) means the algorithm's targeting model was trained on the spend level it's been running at; a sudden cut disturbs that model and it takes a few days to re-settle at the new level, not one overnight reset. Second, conversion lag (10 Jul) means today's orders are still substantially the result of clicks from the last few days - clicks bought *before* the cut. So a spend cut shows up gradually over the following 3-4 days, layered on top of whatever the pre-cut clicks are still converting into, not as an immediate jump in efficiency the very next morning. Where this goes wrong from the outside: someone cuts a budget, checks ROAS the next day, sees no improvement, and concludes the cut "didn't work" - when really it just hasn't had time to.

Worked example, using yesterday's real numbers: PMAX CSS DEC spend fell from GBP 365.84 (26 Jul) to GBP 307.85 (27 Jul). That's 307.85 ÷ 365.84 = 0.842, an 15.8% cut. If cutting worked instantly, you'd expect ROAS to hold steady or improve. Instead overall ROAS moved from 2.46x to 2.31x: 2.31 ÷ 2.46 = 0.939, a 6.1% *worse* result, on 15.8% less money. That's the lag effect in action - yesterday's revenue was still substantially riding on clicks bought at the old, higher spend level from the days before, while the new lower spend level hasn't had its own few days to prove itself yet. It is not evidence the cut was wrong; it's exactly what the lag principle predicts should happen for the first day or two.

**Ask Daryl this:** "When we trim the PMAX daily budget, roughly how many days before you'd expect that to show up as an efficiency improvement in the account, and can you pull the conversion-lag or 'days to convert' report from Google Ads to show me the actual distribution for our campaigns?" A good answer names a specific window (commonly 2-5 days for considered purchases like personalised gifts) and points to real data - the lag/time-to-convert report exists inside Google Ads and Meta Ads Manager and will show it directly. An evasive answer says something like "it should be immediate" or gives no timeframe and no data to back it up - that's a sign the account isn't actually being read at that level of detail.

**You should now be able to:** judge a budget change over a 3-4 day window rather than the very next morning, and recognise a short-term ROAS dip right after a cut as the expected shape of the lag effect rather than proof the cut was a mistake.

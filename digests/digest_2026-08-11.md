## 📚 Lesson of the Day

### Contribution Margin Per Order — The Full Cost Stack

**Builds on:** In June you learned that ROAS measures revenue per pound of ad spend, not profit (Lesson: ROAS vs actual profit margin, 13 Jun). In July you saw contribution after ad spend — total sales minus ad cost — and how that left £1,113 from a £1,655 revenue day (21 Jul). Both are steps toward the same question, but neither actually answers it: **how much money does each order put in the bank?** Today we go the whole way.

**What it is.** Contribution margin per order is the money left after you subtract every cost that fires each time a customer checks out. Not the fixed costs — rent, Shopify subscription, your time — those exist whether you sell zero or a hundred orders. Just the costs that scale with volume. They stack up in layers, and when you see them all together for the first time, the real economics of the business become visible.

Here is the full cost stack for a single Daisy Maison order:

**Layer 1 — Product cost (COGS).** The raw materials and labour to make the item. For a pebble picture: frame, pebbles, backing, glue, any print elements. For a street sign: the blank sign, vinyl, hardware. This is the biggest variable in the stack and the one nobody outside the business knows. It's also the one that determines whether your business model works — everything else is a percentage skimmed off the top, but COGS is the foundation.

**Layer 2 — Packaging.** The box or mailer, tissue paper, branded inserts. Small per unit, but it is real and it scales linearly with orders.

**Layer 3 — Postage cost.** What the Post Office actually charges you, not what the customer paid for shipping. Yesterday the average shipping *revenue* per order was about £5.40 — that is (£2,112 − £1,691) ÷ 78. But that is what came in. What went out to the courier is a different, usually lower, number. The gap between shipping revenue and postage cost is shipping profit — one of the least-discussed revenue lines in e-commerce, and for a small personalised-gift business, it can be meaningful. Since Yodel was dropped and signs now go via Post Office at the same price, your postage cost per parcel is known and stable.

**Layer 4 — Payment processing.** Shopify Payments charges roughly 2% plus 25p per UK card transaction. On yesterday's £27.07 AOV, that is about **£0.79 per order**. Invisible until you look for it, and on 78 orders it totalled roughly £62 skimmed off the top before you saw a penny.

**Layer 5 — Shopify platform fees.** The monthly subscription amortised across orders. On a 78-order day, a £79/month plan costs about £1.01 per order. On a 30-order day, the same plan costs £2.63. This is why volume matters beyond revenue — fixed costs get spread thinner with every extra order.

**Layer 6 — Ad cost per order (CPA).** Yesterday's blended CPA was £718.82 ÷ 78 = **£9.22**. This is the average, but it hides an important split: some of those 78 orders came from organic search and direct traffic and cost zero in ad spend. The true CPA on ad-driven orders is higher — maybe £12–15 — but the blended figure is the one that matters for portfolio decisions because you cannot selectively turn off organic orders to measure the gap.

**The arithmetic, with yesterday's real numbers:**

```
Revenue per order (AOV):            £27.07
Minus COGS:                         − £[you know this]
Minus packaging:                    − £[you know this]
Minus postage cost:                 − £[you know this]
Minus payment processing:           − £0.79
Minus Shopify subscription/order:   − £1.01
Minus blended CPA:                  − £9.22
                                    ─────────
= Contribution margin per order:    £[what's left]
```

If your combined COGS plus packaging plus postage cost runs about £7 per order — a reasonable ballpark for a personalised gift, but only you know the actual number — then:

**£27.07 − £7.00 − £0.79 − £1.01 − £9.22 = £9.05 contribution per order.**

That means yesterday's 78 orders contributed roughly **£706** toward profit and overheads. On a £2,112 revenue day. That ratio — about 33p of every revenue pound actually contributing — is what ROAS alone never tells you.

**Why this matters for ad decisions.** When ROAS is 2.94x and the target is 3x, the instinct is to cut spend. But the real question is: is contribution margin per order still positive? If it is, every order at 2.94x is still making money — just less per pound of spend than at 3x. The cut point — the ROAS below which you are actually losing money on each order — is much lower than 3x. With the numbers above, break-even ROAS would be around 1.6x: the point where CPA equals gross margin per order. Everything above that is profitable. The 2.94x to 3.0x gap is about £0.50 per order of profit difference, not the difference between profit and loss.

**This is why yesterday's 2.94x near-miss is fundamentally different from 28 Jul's 2.00x red day**, where CPA was £14.31 and contribution margin would have been roughly £5 — still positive but getting thin, and the signal that the PMAX scale-up was buying worse customers.

**Where it goes wrong:** If your actual COGS is higher than you think — a common blind spot for businesses that have never itemised their unit costs — the break-even ROAS moves up and what looks like near-miss territory could actually be break-even territory. The only way to know is to calculate it once with real costs, not estimates, and then you have a number you can use every morning.

**ASK DARYL THIS:** "What ROAS do we need to break even after all costs — not just ad spend, but COGS, packing, postage, and Shopify fees? What number does your agency use as the floor?" A good answer names a specific ROAS — probably something like 1.8x–2.2x depending on his margin assumptions — and explains the cost inputs behind it. An evasive answer is "3x is the target" without ever explaining what happens at 2.5x. That means he is managing to a round number rather than the actual economics, and you should be concerned about whether the floor is real or arbitrary.

**You should now be able to:** Calculate the real contribution margin on any day by filling in your actual COGS, packaging, and postage costs — and know whether a sub-3x ROAS day is "less profitable" or "actually losing money."

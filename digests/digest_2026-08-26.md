## 📚 Lesson of the Day

### The Efficiency Curve — why the same spend produces worse results as seasons change, and how to find the spend level this store can sustain right now

**Callback:** You learned about diminishing returns on 28 Jul — the mechanism where each extra pound of ad spend buys a slightly worse customer than the pound before it, because the auction serves the cheapest, most-likely-to-buy people first and the expensive, less-likely people last. Today builds on that. Diminishing returns told you the curve slopes downward. This lesson teaches you the curve itself moves — and how to track where it is right now so you know what to spend.

**The concept: the efficiency curve.** An efficiency curve is the relationship between how much you spend on ads in a day and what ROAS you get back. Plot every day's total spend on the horizontal axis and its ROAS on the vertical axis, and you get a downward-sloping scatter — higher spend days tend to produce lower ROAS. That scatter is not random noise. It traces a curve, and the shape of that curve tells you the maximum you can profitably spend.

But the curve is not fixed. It shifts up and down depending on how much buying demand exists in the market on a given day, week, or season. When demand is strong — Father's Day buildup, teacher gift window, peak wedding season — the curve sits higher. The same £650 of ad spend might produce 3.0x ROAS because lots of people are actively searching for the products you sell, so the algorithm finds buyers easily and cheaply. When demand fades — post-Father's Day, teacher window closed, quieter wedding period — the curve drops. The same £650 now produces 2.2x because fewer people are actively shopping, so the algorithm has to reach further into less-interested audiences to spend your budget, and those people convert at lower rates.

**The mechanism — why the curve shifts.** Google and Facebook's bidding algorithms participate in real-time auctions for each impression. When many shoppers are searching "personalised wedding gift" or scrolling Instagram in a gift-buying mood, there are more high-intent auction opportunities available. Your budget fills up on good clicks. When fewer shoppers are in market, the same budget must fill up somehow — the algorithm doesn't return unspent money (Facebook can spend up to 175% of your daily budget on any given day, and Google up to 200%). It fills by entering lower-quality auctions: broader match queries on Google, less-interested scroll positions on Facebook. The result: same spend, worse traffic, lower CVR, lower ROAS.

**This is exactly what Daisy Maison's data shows.** Consider the last eight weeks of daily data. In early July — peak teacher window plus strong wedding demand — spend at £630–750/day produced ROAS of 2.64x to 3.66x. The 7 Jul Tuesday at £691 spend delivered 3.66x (the best day of the tracked run). Five weeks later, the 25 Aug Tuesday at £643 spend — almost identical money — delivered 2.25x. The spend barely changed. What changed was the demand pool the algorithm could draw from: teacher is dead (zero order-lines since 26 Jul), wedding is in late-summer quiet, and the seasonal tail has shortened the list of high-intent shoppers.

**The worked example — finding your current ceiling.** Take yesterday's numbers. Total spend was **£643.01**. Total sales were **£1,450.07**. ROAS was **2.25x**. Your break-even ROAS (the point where ad spend exactly equals the gross margin it generates) is approximately **1.67x** — that's 1 ÷ 0.60, using the ~60% gross margin on personalised gifts. So yesterday was profitable, but only just: £1,450 revenue generated roughly £870 gross margin, minus £643 ad spend = **£227 contribution** before fees, labour, packaging, and overheads. At your target ROAS of 3.0x, the same £643 spend would have produced £1,929 revenue and £514 contribution — more than double.

Now look at what happens when spend rises. Monday 24 Aug: £740 spend, £1,520 total sales, 2.05x ROAS. The extra £97 in spend (£643 → £740) generated only £70 in additional revenue (£1,450 → £1,520). You paid £97 to get £70 — the marginal ROAS on that £97 was **0.72x**, meaning you lost £27 on it. That is the efficiency curve showing you where the ceiling is. At current demand levels, spending above approximately £620–640/day is buying negative-return traffic.

The reverse also shows: on 22 Aug (a Friday), spend was £618 and ROAS was 2.55x — the best ROAS of the last 8 days, achieved by spending less. Contribution was £618 × 2.55 = £1,576 revenue, yielding £328 contribution — 45% more profit than yesterday on £25 less spend.

**The diagnostic you can run yourself.** Open a spreadsheet with the last 14 days of daily spend and total sales (both numbers are in this digest and its predecessors). Calculate ROAS for each day. Sort by spend ascending. You will see the curve: ROAS generally falls as spend rises, but not uniformly — some high-spend days outperform because of day-of-week demand or a one-off product burst. The trend line through that scatter is your current efficiency curve. Where it crosses 3.0x is your target spend level. Where it crosses 1.67x is your absolute maximum. Right now, the 3.0x crossing appears to be around **£550–580/day**, and the 1.67x crossing is around **£700–720/day**.

I want to see where the efficiency drops off." A good answer is the chart itself, with him pointing to where he'd set the budget. An evasive answer is "ROAS depends on lots of things" without producing the data — which is true but unhelpful, and it's what someone says when they don't want you to see where the curve bends.

**You should now be able to** look at any day's spend and ROAS, compare it to nearby days at different spend levels, and identify whether the store is spending above or below its current efficiency curve — and name the direction the budget should move.

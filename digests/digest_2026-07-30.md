## 📚 Lesson of the Day (confidential — not for Daryl)

### Contribution margin per order

On 13 Jun, the ROAS-versus-profit lesson explained that ROAS only tells you revenue per pound of ad spend, not whether the business actually made money — because product costs, Shopify fees, and packaging eat into the revenue before anything reaches your pocket. On 28 Jun, the CPA lesson gave you the cost to acquire each customer (total spend ÷ total orders). Today's lesson connects those two ideas by building the complete cost stack on a single order, from revenue all the way down to what's actually left over.

**Contribution margin per order** is the amount of money each order puts toward covering your fixed costs — rent, subscriptions, your time, Daryl's GBP 1,000/month — and eventually generating profit, after every variable cost has been deducted. A **variable cost** is one that goes up by a predictable amount every time you sell one more order. The key ones are:

1. **Cost of goods sold (COGS)**: the direct cost to make or source the product — the blank sign, the vinyl or printing, the personalisation labour for each item. This is usually the largest single variable cost.

2. **Packaging and dispatch costs**: box, tissue paper, bubble wrap, label, postage to the courier. This is separate from the shipping fee the customer pays — their GBP 4.95 is revenue; your actual cost to ship the parcel is a variable cost, and the gap between the two is margin.

3. **Payment processing fees**: Shopify Payments takes a cut of every transaction — typically around 1.9% plus 20p per order on the UK Basic plan. On a GBP 30 order, that's roughly 77p. Small per order, but it scales with every sale.

4. **Ad cost per order (CPA)**: the share of ad spend each order carries. Not every order came directly from an ad click (some came through direct or organic search), but since the ad spend is buying the traffic pool that collectively produces the orders, the honest measure is total spend ÷ total orders. This is the variable cost you have the most direct control over — and the one that can swing a profitable day into a loss-making one.

Whatever is left after those four deductions is the **contribution margin**. If it is positive, every order is funding the business. If it is negative, every order is costing you money — more sales makes things worse, not better, because each additional order adds to the loss rather than reducing it. This is the scenario the break-even ROAS concept was built to catch.

**Break-even ROAS** is the ROAS at which contribution margin hits exactly zero — every pound of revenue is consumed by variable costs, and nothing is left over. To find it, you need the **gross margin percentage** — the share of revenue that remains after COGS, packaging, and processing fees, but before ad spend. For a personalised gift business with the shipping fee included in total_sales, a reasonable estimate is around 52-55%. Using 53%:

Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ gross margin = 1 ÷ 0.53 = **1.89x**

At 1.89x, every pound of ad spend generates exactly enough revenue to cover its variable costs and nothing more. Below 1.89x, each order is a net loss. Above it, each order contributes something real. The gap between your actual ROAS and 1.89x is the contribution per pound of spend — and it is not linear. A jump from 2.00x to 2.61x sounds like a 30% improvement, but in terms of actual contribution it is dramatically more, because at 2.00x you are barely clearing the floor.

**Worked example — yesterday versus Tuesday:**

Yesterday (Wed 29 Jul): 56 orders, GBP 1,683.59 total sales, GBP 644.97 ad spend.

- AOV = 1,683.59 ÷ 56 = **GBP 30.06**
- CPA = 644.97 ÷ 56 = **GBP 11.52**
- Estimated COGS + packaging + processing ≈ 47% of revenue = GBP 14.13 per order
- **Contribution margin per order = 30.06 − 14.13 − 11.52 = GBP 4.41**

That is GBP 4.41 going toward rent, subscriptions, Daryl's fee, and profit — from every order. Across 56 orders, that is roughly **GBP 247 of total contribution** for the day.

Now compare with Tuesday (28 Jul): 50 orders, GBP 1,429.91 total sales, GBP 715.47 ad spend.

- AOV = 1,429.91 ÷ 50 = GBP 28.60
- CPA = 715.47 ÷ 50 = **GBP 14.31**
- COGS etc ≈ 47% = GBP 13.44
- **Contribution margin per order = 28.60 − 13.44 − 14.31 = GBP 0.85**

Eighty-five pence per order. The 2.00x ROAS day was not just red — it was barely clearing the break-even floor. Fifty orders generated roughly GBP 43 of total contribution, versus GBP 247 yesterday. The revenue per order was similar (GBP 28.60 vs GBP 30.06 — a GBP 1.46 difference). The variable costs on product, packaging, and fees were similar. The entire swing — GBP 43 versus GBP 247 — came from the ad cost per order: GBP 14.31 versus GBP 11.52. That GBP 2.79 of extra CPA per order, multiplied across 50 orders, wiped out nearly all of Tuesday's economic value.

This is why the PMAX cut mattered urgently: at 2.00x, the store was running at near-zero contribution, and every pound of extra spend above the efficient level was destroying value, not creating it. Yesterday's partial pullback (PMAX down GBP 61 from Tuesday) did not just improve the ROAS number — it moved the per-order economics from "barely surviving" to "meaningfully contributing." The next GBP 50-60 of PMAX cuts toward the GBP 300-310 target should push contribution margin above GBP 5/order.

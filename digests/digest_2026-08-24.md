## Lesson of the Day

### Seasonality Indexing

**Callback:** On 26 June you learned about seasonal demand curves and Google Trends — how search volume for "teacher gift UK" peaks two weeks before end of term, and how launching a campaign before peak demand lets the algorithm finish its learning phase before the wave arrives. Today takes that concept further: instead of eyeballing a single trend line, you learn how to measure what day-of-week and what time of year your store actually trades at, and how to use that measurement to set expectations and budgets.

**What it is.** A seasonality index is a number that tells you how a given day, week, or month compares to the store's own average. An index of 100 means exactly average. An index of 130 means 30% above average. An index of 72 means 28% below average. The word "index" just means a normalised comparison — you are measuring each period against your own baseline, not against an external benchmark.

The simplest version uses day-of-week. Take the last 30 or 60 days of daily revenue, compute the average daily revenue, then compute the average revenue for each weekday. Divide each weekday's average by the overall average and multiply by 100. The result is a set of seven numbers that tells you which days of the week your store naturally runs hot and which run cold.

**Why it matters operationally.** Without a seasonality index, every day is judged against the same target. A Sunday that does £1,671 looks weak against a Monday that did £2,112. But if Sundays naturally index at 85 and Mondays at 110, the Sunday is actually performing at 98% of its expected level (almost bang on) while the Monday is at 96% of its (higher) expected level. Both are performing identically relative to their natural demand — the difference is the day, not the store.

This matters because the wrong diagnosis leads to the wrong action. If you think Sunday underperformed, you might try to push more ad spend on Sundays to "fix" the gap. But if Sundays are naturally quieter (fewer gift-buying occasions, fewer people browsing with purchase intent), pushing more spend into a lower-demand day buys the cheapest, lowest-intent traffic at the worst marginal return. You are spending against the calendar instead of with it.

The same logic applies to weeks and months. August is a transition month for a gift store — summer holidays suppress buying intent for teacher and school-related products (that season ended in July), while wedding season is still live but tapering as the autumn approaches. Christmas planning has not started. The store is in a natural trough between seasonal peaks. Recognising the trough changes the question from "why are sales falling?" (panic framing) to "are sales falling faster than the seasonal pattern predicts?" (diagnostic framing). The first question leads to reflexive budget cuts. The second leads to informed ones.

**The mechanism — why demand is not flat across the week.** Gift purchases are occasion-driven. A personalised Mr & Mrs street sign is not an impulse buy — it is bought for a specific wedding, typically 2–4 weeks out, by someone who has been thinking about it. The decision to buy is often made during the week (browsing at work, seeing an ad on a commute), but the actual purchase happens in the evening or on Saturday when the buyer has time to personalise. This creates a natural weekly cycle: awareness builds Monday–Thursday, conversions cluster Thursday–Saturday, and Sunday dips because fewer new occasions enter the pipeline on a weekend.

Ad platforms amplify this cycle. Facebook and Google both allocate budget within a day based on when they predict the best results. On a day when buying intent is naturally lower (like a Sunday), the algorithm either underspends (if it is smart about it) or buys cheaper, lower-quality clicks to hit its daily budget (if it is optimising for spend delivery rather than outcomes). When you layer a £293 TOF-WEDDING spend on top of a naturally quieter Sunday, you get exactly what happened: more sessions (908 vs Friday's 822), a lower cart-add rate (9.6% vs 12.8%), and a lower CVR (2.64% vs 3.65%). The ad platform filled the session count, but the sessions were worth less because Sunday demand is naturally thinner.

**Worked example — Sunday 23 August.**

Take the last 7 days of total sales (17–23 Aug):

| Day | Total Sales |
|-----|------------|
| Mon 17 | £2,112 |
| Tue 18 | £1,537 |
| Wed 19 | £1,506 |
| Thu 20 | £972 |
| Fri 22 | £1,576 |
| Thu 21 | £1,641 |
| Sun 23 | £1,671 |

Average daily total: £11,016 ÷ 7 = **£1,574**.

Sunday's index: (£1,671 ÷ £1,574) × 100 = **106.2** — meaning Sunday 23 August was actually **6% above this week's daily average**. Against the week, it was a normal-to-good day. It only looks weak when compared to Friday's ROAS, which is the wrong comparison because Friday ran on £618 spend (£92 less) in a demand environment that naturally converts better.

Now compare to the prior Sunday (16 August): £2,189 total. That week's average was £12,659 ÷ 7 = £1,808. Sunday 16 Aug index: (£2,189 ÷ £1,808) × 100 = **121.1**. Last Sunday indexed 21% above its week's average; this Sunday indexed only 6% above. The difference is real — this Sunday underperformed relative to its week by about 15 index points. The question then becomes: is that the extra £120 of spend buying worse traffic, or is Sunday demand genuinely softer this week? The CVR data (2.64% vs 4.68%) points to traffic quality.

**How to build your own.** Open Shopify Analytics → Reports → Sales over time. Set the range to the last 60 days. Group by day-of-week. Export or note the average daily revenue for each weekday. Divide each by the overall average and multiply by 100. You now have seven index numbers. Update them monthly. When you eventually run the ads yourself, use these numbers to set different daily budgets by day of week — higher on indexed-high days (where marginal spend returns are best), lower on indexed-low days (where the same pound buys a worse customer). This is the most basic form of budget pacing, and it is what separates an operator who reacts to yesterday from one who anticipates tomorrow.

The algorithm does differentiate at the auction level, but it does so within whatever daily budget you give it — if you give it £293 on a Sunday, it will spend £293, even if the last £100 is buying the weakest sessions of the week.

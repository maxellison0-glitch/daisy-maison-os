## 📚 Lesson of the Day

### Repeat Purchase Rate — The Number That Tells You Whether CLV Is Real

**Callback:** Back on 22 Jun, the lesson covered Customer Lifetime Value — the idea that a customer who buys once and comes back is worth more than one order's ROAS suggests. That lesson was the concept. Today is the measurement. Because CLV is only worth talking about if you can measure the repeat rate that makes it real.

---

**Repeat purchase rate** is exactly what it sounds like: of all the customers who buy from you in a given window, what percentage have bought from you before? It is the single number that separates "CLV is a nice theory" from "CLV is a real thing that affects how I spend on ads."

Shopify tracks this as the **returning customer rate**: the number of customers in a period who had placed at least one previous order, divided by the total number of customers in that period.

Here is Daisy Maison's real number, pulled live:

- **Last 30 days:** 104 returning customers out of 1,732 total = **6.0% returning customer rate**
- **Yesterday:** 2 returning out of 50 = **4.0%**

That 6% means that roughly **1 in 17 customers** who bought in the last month had bought before. The other 16 were first-timers.

**Why this number matters for ad decisions**

Every ad click you pay for acquires a customer at a cost. Yesterday's CPA (cost per acquisition) was:

> £647.16 spend ÷ 50 orders = **£12.94 per order**

If every customer buys exactly once and never returns, that £12.94 is the full cost of acquiring their revenue. Against yesterday's AOV of £30.19 and assuming ~60% gross margin, that leaves:

> £30.19 × 0.60 = £18.11 gross profit per order
> £18.11 − £12.94 = **£5.17 net contribution per order**

That's positive but thin. Now apply the repeat rate. If 6% of those 50 customers come back and buy again — that's 3 additional future orders you didn't pay to acquire. Those 3 orders arrive at **zero CPA** because the customer already exists in your email list, already knows the brand, and returns on their own (via direct or search, not a paid ad). Each repeat order at £30 AOV and 60% margin contributes £18.11 in gross profit with no ad cost against it.

The arithmetic:

> 50 orders × £5.17 net contribution = £258.50 from first purchases
> 3 repeat orders × £18.11 gross profit (zero ad cost) = £54.33 from repeats
> **Total value of yesterday's cohort: £312.83, not £258.50**

That extra £54 is invisible in yesterday's ROAS. The 2.33x is calculated on day-of-sale revenue only. The repeat purchases land weeks or months later and show up as "organic" or "direct" revenue that appears to have no ad cost against it — but it was the original ad spend that created the customer.

**Why 6% is low, and what moves it**

A 6% returning customer rate for a personalised gifts store is below the Shopify average (~20–25% for established DTC brands). That's not surprising — gift purchases are occasion-driven (weddings, christenings, teacher end-of-term), so the buyer often has no natural second occasion in the near future. But a subset of your customers DO have repeat occasions:

- A wedding buyer who comes back for a christening gift 18 months later
- A customer who bought for one friend's wedding and returns when another friend gets married
- Someone who bought Mr & Mrs and comes back for a Family Blossom Tree for their own home
- Christmas gift buyers who return for Valentine's or Mother's Day

The levers that move repeat rate are **email capture** (are you collecting emails from all 50 of yesterday's buyers?), **post-purchase flows** (a well-timed "we noticed you bought a wedding gift — here are our christening and anniversary ranges" email 60 days later), and **brand recognition** (does the packaging say "Daisy Maison" prominently enough that they remember where to come back?).

**The failure mode**

The failure looks like this from the outside: you're spending £647/day on ads, ROAS is a thin 2.33x, and you think "I'm barely making money on these customers." So you cut spend. Orders drop. But what you also cut, invisibly, was the pipeline of future repeat buyers. Three months later, your "organic" revenue — the direct and search traffic that converts without ad spend — starts drifting down, and you don't connect it to the ad cut because the lag is too long. That's the repeat-rate effect working in reverse.

**A worked caution:** at 6% repeat rate, repeat purchases add roughly 6% to the value of each cohort. At 15%, they add 15%. The difference between 6% and 15% on yesterday's numbers is:

> At 6%: 3 repeat orders × £18.11 = £54.33 bonus
> At 15%: 7.5 repeat orders × £18.11 = £135.83 bonus

That £81 difference per day, compounded over a year, is roughly **£29,500** in additional gross profit from the same ad spend. The ad spend doesn't change — only the post-purchase relationship does.

**ASK DARYL THIS:** "What's our repeat purchase rate by acquisition channel — do customers acquired through Facebook ads come back at the same rate as ones who found us through Google Shopping?" A good answer names the rates by channel and whether email flows are set up to re-engage by occasion. An evasive answer talks about "brand awareness" without numbers. The reason this matters: if Facebook-acquired customers repeat at 3% and Google-acquired at 9%, the Google CPA is even more justified than the day-of ROAS suggests, and the Facebook spend needs post-purchase email support to earn its keep.

**You should now be able to:** look at a red ROAS day and ask "yes, but how many of these customers will come back?" — and know that the answer is currently about 6%, that this is lower than ideal, and that improving it through email and packaging is the cheapest growth lever after CVR.

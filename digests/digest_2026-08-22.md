## 📚 Lesson of the Day

### PMax Campaign Rebuilds — What PMAX CSS AUG Means and Why Campaigns Get Replaced

**Callback:** On 7 Jun you learned what Google Performance Max is — a single campaign type that runs ads across every Google surface (Search, Shopping, Display, YouTube, Gmail, Maps) using one pool of assets, with Google's algorithm deciding which surface, audience, and bid to use for each impression. That lesson covered asset groups (the containers for your product images, headlines, descriptions, and audience signals) and why PMax has been the most stable campaign in the Daisy account — steady spend, consistent CPC, predictable delivery. Today's lesson is about what happens when that stability reaches its limits and the campaign needs to be rebuilt from scratch.

**What happened yesterday:** A new campaign called **[RDD] TOP PRODUCTS - PMAX CSS - AUG** appeared in the Google Ads account for the first time, spending £225.51 on 266 clicks. The existing **PMAX CSS - DEC** campaign — the same one that has run since at least December 2025 and has been the primary Google spend vehicle for the entire tracked run — dropped from £322.53 to £149.36 in a single day. This is a campaign rebuild: Daryl has created a fresh PMax campaign to replace the old one.

**Why agencies rebuild rather than edit.** Every PMax campaign accumulates what Google calls "learning data" — a model of which audiences, placements, times of day, and device types convert best for the products in the campaign. This model is built from thousands of auctions over weeks and months, and it is what makes PMax "smart": the algorithm has seen which kinds of people buy your Mr & Mrs signs, at what time, on which device, from which location. That accumulated model is the campaign's memory.

The problem is that memory can become a liability. The data from December 2025 taught the algorithm about a store that was running Christmas and New Year gift campaigns, targeting Christmas shoppers, with a Christmas product mix. By August 2026, the store is entirely wedding-focused, teacher gifts have come and gone, the product catalogue has changed, and the people searching for personalised gifts are a different demographic with different intent. The algorithm is still partly optimising based on what worked eight months ago. It is like navigating with a map from last winter — the roads haven't moved, but the traffic patterns have.

Editing the existing campaign — changing the asset groups, swapping products, updating audience signals — does reset the learning phase for those specific components, but the campaign-level historical data (the broad model of "who converts for this campaign") carries forward. A brand-new campaign starts with no historical baggage: the algorithm builds its targeting model from scratch using only the current product feed, current creative, and current audience signals. The tradeoff is clear: you lose the accumulated learning (which was going stale anyway) and gain a fresh model trained on current conditions.

**How a rebuild transition works.** The standard practice is a parallel run: keep the old campaign active at reduced budget while the new one ramps up through its learning phase (typically 2–4 weeks for PMax). The old campaign continues to deliver during the transition — you don't want a gap in Shopping coverage while the new campaign finds its feet. Once the new campaign exits learning and delivers comparable or better results, the old one is paused entirely. The "DEC" and "AUG" suffixes in the campaign names are the dating convention — they tell you when each campaign was built, which is useful when you have multiple campaigns in different lifecycle stages.

**What to watch during the transition.** Three things:

First, **total combined spend.** Yesterday, PMAX CSS DEC (£149.36) plus PMAX CSS AUG (£225.51) equals **£374.87** total Google PMax CSS spend. That is higher than DEC was running on its own (£322.53 on 20 Aug, ~£340 for most of the last month). A rebuild should not be a stealth budget increase — the combined cap should match or beat the old single-campaign budget until the new campaign proves itself.

Second, **CPC during learning.** The new PMAX CSS AUG delivered £0.85 CPC yesterday versus DEC's £0.88. On day one that is neutral — no signal yet. During a PMax learning phase, CPC can spike as the algorithm experiments with different auction segments. Watch for PMAX CSS AUG's CPC climbing above £1.00 in the first week — that would be normal learning-phase exploration, not a problem, but it means early ROAS will look worse before it improves.

Third, **duplicate targeting.** Two PMax campaigns with overlapping product feeds can bid against each other in the same auction. Google's auction-deduplication logic is supposed to prevent this, but it is imperfect. If both campaigns are drawing from the same product feed (likely — both say "TOP PRODUCTS"), some auctions will see bids from both, driving up the CPC for that product. The diagnostic is whether combined CPC (across both campaigns) rises above the historical DEC-only CPC over the first week.

**Worked example from yesterday's numbers.** PMAX CSS DEC spent £322.53 on 20 Aug for 358 clicks at £0.90 CPC. Yesterday, the two campaigns combined spent £374.87 for 436 clicks at an average of £0.86 CPC (£374.87 ÷ 436). More clicks at a cheaper average CPC is the right direction. But the combined spend is £52 higher than DEC alone was running. If the combined budget had been capped at £320 (the recommendation) and split between the two campaigns, the 436 clicks would have cost £277.76 at the same £0.86 average — saving £97 on the day while still transitioning the campaigns. At 2.33x ROAS, that £97 saving would have cost roughly £226 in attributed revenue (£97 × 2.33) — which sounds like a loss until you realise that marginal clicks are the worst-performing ones (the diminishing returns lesson from 28 Jul), so the real revenue loss from cutting the last £97 of spend is lower than the average suggests.

**ASK DARYL THIS:** "What prompted the new PMAX CSS AUG campaign — was it audience exhaustion on DEC, a seasonal asset group refresh, or a product feed change? Is DEC being wound down completely, and over what timeline? And should the combined PMax CSS budget across both campaigns be capped at £280-320/day during the transition, or are you deliberately scaling Google while Facebook stays flat?" A good answer names the specific reason for the rebuild and gives a timeline for sunsetting DEC. An evasive answer is "I'm testing something" with no specifics — that means Daryl doesn't want to explain the strategy, which is fine, but you need to know the combined budget target so you can track whether it's being hit.

On TikTok Ads, the equivalent situation would be duplicating a campaign (TikTok's "Copy" function) to reset the learning phase — TikTok's algorithm also accumulates historical data that can go stale, and the fix is the same: fresh campaign, reduced-budget parallel run, sunset the old one once the new one exits learning.

**You should now be able to** spot when a new PMax campaign appears alongside an old one, understand whether it is a planned rebuild or accidental duplication, and know what questions to ask about combined budget, timeline, and targeting overlap during the transition.

## 📚 Lesson of the Day

### Contribution Margin Per Order

**Building on:** On 13 Jun you learned why ROAS alone does not confirm profitability — you need to know your break-even ROAS, which is 1 divided by your gross margin percentage. On 21 Jul you learned what remains after subtracting ad spend from revenue (contribution after ad spend). Today takes both ideas a step further and asks the question that actually determines whether the business grew yesterday: after you pay for the product, the packaging, the postage, Shopify's cut, and the ad click that brought the customer in — how much money does each order actually leave in the bank?

**The concept.** Contribution margin per order is the amount of money left over from a single order after you subtract every *variable* cost directly tied to fulfilling it. "Variable" means the cost only happens because the order happened — if there were no order, you would not pay it. Fixed costs like your Shopify subscription, your phone bill, or Daryl's monthly fee are not included, because you pay those regardless of whether you sell zero orders or a hundred. Contribution margin isolates the profit each individual order generates before overheads.

The variable costs on a typical Daisy Maison order are:

- **Product cost** — the materials and production cost of the item itself. For a personalised street sign: the wood blank, the vinyl, the engraving or printing time. Roughly £3–5.
- **Packaging** — the box, tissue paper, protective wrapping. Roughly £1–2.
- **Postage** — what you pay the Post Office or courier to move the parcel. For a sign, roughly £3–4 at Post Office rates.
- **Payment processing** — Shopify Payments takes approximately 2% of the order value. The exact rate varies by plan, but 2% is close enough for daily estimation.
- **Ad cost per order** — your total daily ad spend divided by your total daily orders. This is the CPA (cost per acquisition) you learned about on 28 Jun, applied backwards to each order.

Add those up, subtract from the order's total revenue, and what remains is the contribution margin. Every penny of it either covers your fixed overheads or, once those are covered, is profit.

**Why this matters more than ROAS.** ROAS tells you one thing: how many pounds of revenue each pound of ad spend generates. Yesterday's 2.98x means every £1 of ads generated £2.98 of revenue. But it says nothing about how much of that £2.98 is consumed by the costs of actually making and shipping the product. Two days can produce identical ROAS but leave very different amounts of money in the business if the product mix changes — and the biggest variable in the mix is add-ons, because add-ons carry almost no cost at all.

A Size Upgrade — Large adds £8.99 to the order. But the cost of fulfilling it is essentially zero: no extra materials (the sign is just cut larger from the same blank), no extra box (it ships in the same package), no extra postage (same weight class), and no extra ad spend (the CPA was already paid by the base product). The only cost is payment processing at ~2%, or about 18p. That leaves £8.81 of the £8.99 as pure contribution. An add-on is not a bonus — it is the margin.

**Worked example with yesterday's numbers.**

Yesterday: **£1,839.16 total sales**, **65 orders**, **£618.07 ad spend**.

Step 1 — CPA: £618.07 ÷ 65 = **£9.51** per order. That is what the business paid in ads to generate each sale.

Step 2 — Take a typical Mr & Mrs Street Sign order with a Size Upgrade Large and Mounting Strips:

| Line | Revenue |
|---|---|
| Base sign | £11.25 |
| Size Upgrade Large | £8.99 |
| Mounting Strips | £1.99 |
| Shipping (customer pays) | ~£4.95 |
| **Order total** | **~£27.18** |

Variable costs to fulfil:

| Cost | Amount |
|---|---|
| Product (sign, materials) | ~£4.00 |
| Packaging | ~£1.50 |
| Postage (Post Office) | ~£3.50 |
| Payment processing (~2%) | ~£0.54 |
| Ad cost (CPA) | £9.51 |
| **Total variable cost** | **~£19.05** |

**Contribution margin: ~£8.13** — about **30%** of the order value.

Step 3 — Now isolate the add-ons. The Size Upgrade Large (£8.99) cost: payment processing ~£0.18. Everything else is zero. Contribution: **£8.81**. The Mounting Strips (£1.99) cost: payment processing ~£0.04, product cost perhaps £0.10 for the actual strips. Contribution: ~**£1.85**.

Without those two add-ons, the order's contribution margin would have been roughly £8.13 − £8.81 − £1.85 = **negative £2.53**. The base sign order, on its own at yesterday's CPA, would have *lost money*. The add-ons are what flipped it from loss to profit.

Step 4 — Scale that across the day. Yesterday's core upsell revenue was **£299.97** across 51 units. If the average contribution margin on add-ons is ~95% (near-zero product cost), that is roughly **£285 of pure contribution** flowing straight to the bottom line. Without add-on revenue, total sales would have been ~£1,539, making ROAS just **2.49x** — firmly red and looking like a problem day. The add-ons moved ROAS by **0.49 points**. They are the difference between a worrying red day and a near-miss that needs no action.

**Ask Daryl this:** "When you evaluate whether to scale a campaign's budget, do you look at the product mix it drives — specifically whether those orders carry add-ons — or just the headline ROAS? Because two campaigns can produce identical 3x ROAS but very different profitability if one drives more add-on-heavy orders. Can you pull which campaigns generate the most orders with size upgrades attached?" A good answer shows he thinks about order quality and margin, not just the top-line revenue number. An evasive answer talks about ROAS targets and "overall performance" without acknowledging that what is inside each order matters as much as how many orders there are.

**You should now be able to:** look at any day's ROAS result and ask the next question — not just "did we hit 3x?" but "after product costs, postage, and fees, did each order actually leave money in the business?" The upsell tracker is not a nice-to-have reporting section. It is the margin.

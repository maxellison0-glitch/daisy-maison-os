## 📚 Lesson of the Day

### Standard Events and the Pixel — How Facebook Knows What Counts as a Sale

**Callback:** In the Retargeting lesson (21 Jun), you learned that the Facebook Pixel records every visitor and builds warm audience pools for retargeting campaigns. Today goes one layer deeper: what exactly does the pixel record at each step, and how does Facebook use those signals to decide who sees your ad?

**The pixel fires named events, not just "visits."**

When someone browses daisymaisonuk.myshopify.com, the Facebook Pixel fires a specific named signal at each stage of their journey. These are Standard Events:

1. **ViewContent** — someone looked at a product page. Fires on every product URL.
2. **AddToCart** — someone added a product to their cart. A stronger signal: this person is shopping.
3. **InitiateCheckout** — someone started the checkout process.
4. **Purchase** — someone completed a purchase. This carries the order value (e.g. "Purchase, value: £28.57"). This is the signal the algorithm cares most about.

Each event fires in real time from ALL traffic sources — not just Facebook ads. When a Google Shopping visitor buys, the pixel still records that Purchase event. Facebook accumulates every event and builds a statistical profile of "a person who buys from Daisy Maison."

**How the algorithm uses these events.**

When an ad set's Optimisation Goal is set to OFFSITE_CONVERSIONS — which all three of your active Facebook ad sets use — you are telling Facebook: "Show my ads to the people most likely to fire a Purchase event on my store."

Facebook works backwards from your Purchase events. It examines the last several hundred people who bought and maps their characteristics: demographics, interests, online behaviour patterns, time of day they buy, device type, scroll speed. It builds a composite "buyer profile" and then scores every person in your target audience against it. Higher match score = more likely to see your ad.

This is why marketers say "the pixel needs data." Without enough Purchase events, the profile is blurry and targeting is essentially random.

**The 50-event threshold.**

Facebook states that an ad set needs approximately 50 optimisation events per week to maintain "stable delivery." Below 50, the algorithm oscillates — it experiments with different audience segments, and performance swings daily.

Yesterday, Daisy Maison had 55 orders. Over the last 7 days: 339 orders. The pixel saw all 339 Purchase events regardless of traffic source, giving the algorithm strong signal. But the threshold applies per ad set. If three ad sets each generate only 10–15 purchases per week, each one's algorithm starves even though the pixel is healthy overall. This is one reason consolidating ad sets often outperforms splitting into many small ones.

**The three failure modes.**

1. **Wrong optimisation event.** If an ad set optimises for Link Clicks instead of Purchases, Facebook finds people who click — not people who buy. These are different populations. Click-optimised audiences produce high CTR, low CVR, cheap CPC. The algorithm is working exactly as instructed: finding clickers, not buyers. This is the "cheap click trap" from the Traffic Quality lesson (20 Aug), explained at the pixel level.

2. **Broken pixel.** If the Purchase event stops firing — after a theme update, a checkout plugin change, or a Shopify app conflict — the algorithm loses its feedback loop. It sends traffic, conversions happen, but the pixel never reports them. Facebook thinks the campaign stopped converting and starts exploring new audiences, driving CPC up. A pixel outage of 2–3 days can take a week to recover.

3. **Insufficient volume.** A new ad set starts with zero Purchase events. The first ~50 purchases are the "learning phase" — the algorithm is building its buyer profile from scratch. CPC is typically 20–40% higher than steady state. This is exactly what PMAX CSS AUG shows on the Google side (day 10, CPC £0.70, settling toward steady state).

**Worked example.**

Yesterday's two TOF-WEDDING ad sets both optimise for Purchases. Their click metrics diverge sharply:

- **Copy**: 442 clicks, £0.41 CPC, 0.91% CTR
- **more**: 263 clicks, £0.19 CPC, 2.61% CTR

"Copy" has worse click metrics everywhere. But both optimise for Purchases, not clicks. The algorithm behind "Copy" might be finding people who click less readily but buy more reliably — higher conversion rate per click, lower cost per purchase, despite a higher CPC. The click metrics alone cannot tell you which ad set is winning.

The deciding metric is **Cost Per Purchase** at the ad set level: spend ÷ purchases. If "Copy" spent £179 and generated 12 purchases (£14.92 each), while "more" spent £50 and generated 4 purchases (£12.50 each), then "more" is more efficient per purchase but "Copy" generates 3× the volume at a 19% premium — a reasonable scale trade-off. If "Copy" generated only 5 purchases (£35.80 each), it is burning money. Windsor cannot break out purchases by ad set. Ads Manager can.

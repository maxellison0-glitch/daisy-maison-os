## 📚 Lesson of the Day

### Contribution Margin Per Order

**Callback:** On 13 June you learned the break-even ROAS formula — 1 ÷ gross margin — which told you the minimum ROAS to avoid selling at a loss on product cost alone. On 21 July you learned contribution after ad spend — subtracting total ad spend from total revenue to see what the store kept after paying for traffic. Today goes one level deeper: what actually lands in the bank from each individual order after every variable cost, not just ads.

**What it is.** Contribution margin per order is the money left over from a single order after you subtract every cost that only exists because that order happened. If the order had not been placed, these costs would not have been incurred. The word "contribution" means: what this order contributes toward covering your fixed costs — rent, subscriptions, Daryl's £1k/month, your time — and, after those are covered, toward actual profit.

The variable costs that attach to each order are:

1. **COGS (cost of goods sold)** — the raw materials, personalisation labour, and production time for the items in that order. A Mr & Mrs Large street sign has a different COGS than a pebble picture. This is the number you know from running the workshop; it is not visible anywhere in Shopify.
2. **CPA (cost per acquisition)** — total ad spend ÷ total orders. This is your share of the day's advertising bill allocated to one order. It is the only variable cost that moves with your ad efficiency.
3. **Payment processing** — Shopify Payments charges approximately 2.9% of the transaction value plus £0.20 per transaction (varies by plan). On a £29.19 order, that is about £1.05.
4. **Packaging and materials** — boxes, tissue paper, labels, ink cartridges. These cost something per parcel. You know the figure.
5. **Postage** — the actual cost of sending the parcel, distinct from the shipping charge the customer pays. Stable since the Post Office switch.

**Why it matters more than ROAS.** ROAS measures revenue per pound of ad spend. A 2.55x ROAS sounds like £1.55 of value created per pound spent. But that £1.55 is revenue, not profit — it still has to pay for the product, the packaging, and the fees before anything reaches the bank. Contribution margin strips all of that away and shows the actual cash generated per order. A day can look fine on ROAS and be marginal on contribution margin, which is exactly what happens when CPA creeps up on sub-3x days.

**Why CPA dominates.** Most variable costs are fixed per order — COGS, packaging, and fees do not change when your ads get more expensive. CPA is the exception. When ROAS drops from 3.0x to 2.55x, CPA absorbs all that volatility because it is the only variable cost that moves with ad spend. This makes contribution margin extremely sensitive to ROAS in a narrow band — the band the store has been sitting in for the last fourteen days.

**Worked example — Friday 22 August.**

Total sales: **£1,576.26** across **54 orders** → AOV **£29.19**.
Total ad spend: **£617.76** → CPA = £617.76 ÷ 54 = **£11.44**.

Using the ~60% gross margin assumption from the 13 June lesson (meaning ~40% of revenue goes to product costs — this is an estimate; you know the real figure from the workshop):

| Cost component | Per order |
|----------------|----------|
| Revenue (AOV) | £29.19 |
| − COGS (~40%) | −£11.68 |
| − CPA | −£11.44 |
| − Payment processing (~2.9% + £0.20) | −£1.05 |
| − Packaging (estimate) | −£1.50 |
| **= Contribution margin** | **£3.52** |

Total contribution for the day: £3.52 × 54 = **£190.08**.

Now the same arithmetic at **3.0x ROAS** (the target floor). Same revenue requires spend of £1,576 ÷ 3.0 = £525. CPA = £525 ÷ 54 = **£9.73**.

| | At 2.55x (actual) | At 3.0x (target) |
|--|-------------------|------------------|
| CPA | £11.44 | £9.73 |
| Contribution per order | £3.52 | £5.23 |
| Daily total | £190 | £282 |

The gap: **£92 per day** from a 0.45x ROAS improvement. Over a month that is approximately **£2,770** of real cash — the cost of the 14-day red streak in a single number. On the worst day of the run (20 August, 1.54x ROAS, CPA = £629.57 ÷ 34 = £18.52), contribution margin was approximately **−£3.56 per order** — every order the store fulfilled that day cost money to process.

**Why add-ons change the picture.** The £341.94 of core add-on revenue (26.4% of gross) has near-zero COGS — a mounting strip costs pennies, a size upgrade is the same wood with a slightly larger template, and gift wrap is tissue paper and ribbon. Almost every pound of add-on revenue flows directly into contribution margin. Without add-ons, gross sales per order would drop from £23.97 to roughly £17.64 (subtracting the £341.94 across 54 orders), and contribution margin at today's CPA would be approximately **−£3.82 per order** — deeply negative. The add-ons are not a nice-to-have. They are the mechanism that keeps the per-order economics viable at sub-3x ROAS. This is why the upsell tracker exists and why every new add-on product matters more than it looks from a top-line view.

I need to know whether the bid strategy is set to a number that covers our actual product costs, or whether it is running on the platform default." If the tROAS is set below about 2.5x given your cost structure, every order the algorithm wins at that price is a contribution-negative order.

Write it down.

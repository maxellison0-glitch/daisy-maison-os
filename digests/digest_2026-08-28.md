## 📚 Lesson of the Day

### Budget Allocation Under a Constraint — How to Split a Fixed Daily Budget Across Campaigns

Yesterday's lesson introduced the efficiency curve — the spend level where ROAS crosses 3.0x and the level where it crosses break-even (~1.67x). Today builds directly on that: once you know your total budget ceiling, how do you decide which campaigns get the money?

**The concept: marginal allocation.** When you have a fixed amount to spend each day — say £550, which is where the efficiency curve says this store's 3.0x crossing sits — every pound you give to Campaign A is a pound Campaign B does not get. The question is not "is this campaign profitable?" (that is blended ROAS, taught 6 Jul). The question is "would this pound produce more revenue here or in the other campaign?" That is a marginal allocation decision, and it is the single most important skill an in-house ad operator uses.

**How it works in practice.** You have four active campaigns across two platforms:

1. **PMAX CSS AUG** (Google, £330/day) — Performance Max, the algorithmic black box that targets Shopping and other placements. You cannot control which products or search terms get the budget within PMax; Google's algorithm decides. Your only lever is the daily budget and any tROAS target.

2. **All Other CSS** (Google, £77/day) — a supplementary Shopping campaign that catches overflow queries PMax misses. Lower spend, similar CPC, steady performer.

3. **TOF-WEDDING** (Facebook, £213/day) — a top-of-funnel campaign designed to find new potential buyers through Facebook and Instagram. It generates cheap clicks (£0.32 CPC) and lots of impressions, but these are cold-audience sessions that convert at a materially lower rate than search traffic.

4. **Social Engagement** (Facebook, £23/day) — a small brand-awareness campaign.

A naive allocation says "give the most money to the campaign with the best ROAS." But you cannot measure per-campaign ROAS from Shopify because Shopify does not attribute orders to specific campaigns — it only sees the last click before purchase. A Facebook TOF click on Monday might create a Google search visit on Wednesday that Shopify attributes to Google. This is why the digest uses Shopify total_sales ÷ total spend as the ROAS, not platform-reported numbers.

**So how do you allocate?** You use three signals together:

1. **The platform's CPC relative to CVR.** Google's CPC is £0.79 but search traffic converts at approximately 3–4% (shoppers with intent). Facebook's CPC is £0.32 but social traffic converts at approximately 1–2% (browsers without intent). A £0.79 click that converts 3.5% of the time costs £0.79 / 0.035 = **£22.57 per order**. A £0.32 click that converts 1.5% costs £0.32 / 0.015 = **£21.33 per order**. At these rates, the cost per order is similar — but the Google orders are more certain (search intent) and the Facebook orders include substantial conversion lag (clicks today, orders in 3 days), making Facebook's true CPA hard to measure on any single day.

2. **The diminishing returns curve.** Each campaign has a point where adding more budget buys worse and worse traffic. PMAX CSS AUG at £280/day was recommended as the efficient level; at £330 it is buying clicks at £0.80 that Google's algorithm considers less likely to convert. TOF-WEDDING at £125/day was recommended because beyond that, Facebook exhausts the high-quality wedding-interested audience and starts serving to people who will never buy a personalised gift.

3. **The role in the funnel.** TOF-WEDDING's real job is not to produce orders directly — it is to fill the pixel pool with visitors so that retargeting campaigns (like TEST OCT 2022, which has been absent for a week) can convert them later. Without the retargeting layer running, TOF-WEDDING's cold traffic has no downstream conversion engine. Spending £213/day on top-of-funnel when the retargeting campaign that monetises it has been offline for 7 days is like filling a reservoir that has no tap.

**The worked example from yesterday.** Total spend: £643. The efficiency curve says the ceiling should be approximately £555. The £88 to cut comes from the campaign with the worst marginal return. PMAX is in learning phase (disrupting it costs more than overspending by £50), so the cut comes from TOF-WEDDING: £213 → £125 = £88 saved. The maths:

- At £643 spend: ROAS = £1,163 / £643 = **1.81x** (near break-even)
- If TOF-WEDDING had been capped at £125 (total spend £555): the £88 saved would have bought approximately 275 clicks at £0.32 CPC, converting at ~1.5% = ~4 orders at ~£29 AOV = ~£116. So the lost revenue is approximately £116, giving estimated ROAS of (£1,163 − £116) / £555 = £1,047 / £555 = **1.89x**. Better, not great — but the loss is smaller than the savings.
- The real upside is that reducing TOF-WEDDING spend concentrates Facebook's remaining £125 on the highest-quality segment of the wedding audience instead of scraping the bottom of the barrel at the margin.

**The failure mode to watch:** over-cutting to chase ROAS. If you cut TOF-WEDDING to zero, you lose all new-audience prospecting, the pixel pool stops refreshing, and when TEST OCT 2022 comes back online, it has no warm audience to retarget. The £125 floor is not arbitrary — it is roughly 50 purchase events per month at current CVR, which keeps the campaign in a functioning state and feeds downstream retargeting. Cut below that and the algorithm drops out of optimisation.

**Where the controls are.** In Facebook Ads Manager: open the campaign, click into the ad set level, find Budget & Schedule, enable Ad Set Spend Limits, set Maximum Daily Spend to £125. In Google Ads: open the PMAX CSS AUG campaign, click the pencil icon next to the daily budget, type £300 (or whatever the cap is). These are two 30-second changes that would have saved approximately £1,605 over the last 18 days on TOF-WEDDING alone.

**TikTok note:** TikTok Ads Manager has the same structure — Campaign → Ad Group (their term for ad set) → Ad. Budget lives at either level. The minimum daily budget is typically $20/£16. Same marginal allocation principle applies.

**ASK DARYL THIS:** "What is the optimisation event set on TOF-WEDDING — is it optimising for link clicks, landing page views, or purchases? And if it's link clicks, can we switch it to purchase optimisation so Facebook targets buyers instead of clickers?" A good answer names the specific event and explains why. An evasive answer is "it's set up correctly" without naming it. If it is optimising for link clicks, that explains the high volume of cheap, non-converting sessions, and switching to purchase optimisation is the highest-leverage single change available.

**You should now be able to:** look at a set of campaigns with a fixed total budget, identify which one is past its diminishing-returns point, and reallocate the excess to the campaign (or to savings) that produces the better marginal result — rather than spreading money evenly or giving the most to the cheapest-click campaign.

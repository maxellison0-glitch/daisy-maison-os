## 📚 Lesson of the Day

### Returning Customer Rate and First-Purchase Economics

**Builds on:** CPA (28 Jun) and Customer Lifetime Value (22 Jun). Those lessons showed you how much it costs to win a customer and why — in theory — a customer who comes back multiple times can justify a higher first-purchase cost. Today's lesson asks the practical question: *do* your customers come back?

---

**Returning customer rate** is simply the percentage of today's orders placed by people who have bought from you before. Shopify tracks this automatically. A high returning rate means your ad spend is partially an investment — you pay once, earn twice or more. A low returning rate means each order stands alone: the ad cost, the product margin, the add-on revenue, and the profit all have to work on that single transaction. There's no future purchase coming to rescue a thin first order.

Yesterday, **2 of 72 customers were returning buyers. That's a 2.8% returning customer rate.** The other 70 people had never bought from Daisy Maison before.

This isn't unusual — in fact, it's *expected* for a personalised gift store. Your products are bought for occasions: weddings, christenings, birthdays, teacher thank-yous. These are one-off events. Someone buying a Mr & Mrs Street Sign for a wedding isn't buying another one next month. Unlike a consumable brand (coffee, skincare, pet food) where the same customer reorders every few weeks, Daisy Maison's revenue comes overwhelmingly from finding *new* people who are experiencing *their* occasion right now. The Facebook and Google ads aren't farming a loyal base — they're fishing in open water every single day.

**Why this matters for how you read ROAS:** When the CLV lesson (22 Jun) introduced the idea that a customer might be worth more than their first purchase, the assumption was that some portion of today's buyers would return. At 2.8%, that assumption is nearly zero. The practical implication is sharp: **your break-even ROAS calculation cannot include future-purchase value, because there almost isn't any.** Every order must pay for itself on the day.

Here's the arithmetic from yesterday, which makes the point concrete:

- **Total ad spend:** £713.96
- **Orders:** 72
- **CPA (cost per acquisition):** £713.96 ÷ 72 = **£9.92 per customer**
- **Gross revenue per order:** £1,501.75 ÷ 72 = **£20.86**

Now estimate the margin. Assume product cost of goods is roughly 40% of gross revenue (materials, production, packing — this is an estimate, and the biggest unknown is the per-unit production cost on personalised items, which varies by product). That gives:

- **Gross profit per order:** £20.86 × 60% = **£12.52**
- **After ad cost:** £12.52 − £9.92 = **£2.60 contribution per order**

On 72 orders, that's **£187 of contribution** for the day — the money left after product costs and ad spend, before fixed overheads (rent, subscriptions, Daryl's fee, Shopify plan).

Now strip out the add-ons and watch what happens. Yesterday's core upsell revenue was £266.01 (strips, size upgrades, easel, gift wrap). Add-ons cost almost nothing to produce — a mounting strip is pennies, a gift wrap kit is a bag and tissue paper. Call add-on margin 90%. Without them:

- **Base product gross per order:** (£1,501.75 − £266.01) ÷ 72 = **£17.16**
- **Base product gross profit:** £17.16 × 60% = **£10.30**
- **After ad cost:** £10.30 − £9.92 = **£0.38 per order**

Thirty-eight pence. On a day with 72 orders. That's **£27 of daily contribution** — not enough to cover a single day of Shopify's plan fee.

The add-ons took contribution per order from £0.38 to £2.60 — a **7x lift**. They turned a day that would barely break even into one that cleared £187 after ad and product costs. In a business where 97% of customers never return, add-ons aren't a nice enhancement. They are the margin model.

This is why the upsell tracker section exists in every digest, why the easel launch mattered, why the £1.99 strips rollback was correct (volume at near-100% margin beats a higher price with fewer attaches), and why every new add-on that increases items-per-order is worth more than an equivalent amount of ad spend. An add-on attached to an existing order has **zero customer acquisition cost** — the ad already paid for the visit. Every penny of add-on margin is pure.

**There's a second implication** that feeds directly into ad strategy. If customers don't return, the only way to grow revenue is to find *more* new customers — which means more ad spend. But more spend hits diminishing returns (28 Jul lesson): each extra pound buys a slightly worse customer. You're running on a treadmill where growth = more spend = higher CPA = thinner margin per order. The escape from the treadmill is raising the value of each visit through add-ons, not running the treadmill faster through more budget.

**ASK DARYL THIS:** "What's the returning customer rate inside the Facebook ad account — is any campaign generating repeat buyers, or are they all first-touch acquisition?" A good answer would reference the Facebook Ads Manager's customer type breakdown (new vs returning) and whether any campaign shows a meaningful returning-customer rate. An evasive answer would be "the pixel tracks that" without giving you the number. What you're really asking is whether any of the £294/day Facebook spend is going toward people who already know the brand and would have bought anyway — because if so, that spend is inflating attributed ROAS without creating real new revenue.

**You should now be able to** look at the returning customer rate in Shopify analytics, understand why it's low for this type of business, and explain why that makes the add-on strategy structural rather than optional.

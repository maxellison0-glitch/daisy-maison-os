### 📚 Lesson of the Day

#### The Google Ads Optimization Score and Recommendations Tab

**Callback:** In the Change History lesson (25 Aug), you learned where to find what has already changed in your account — the timestamped log of every edit, who made it, and what the old and new values were. Today is the other side of that coin: the Recommendations Tab is where Google tries to persuade you to make changes before they happen. Some of those suggestions are genuinely useful. Others are designed to increase your spend — and therefore Google's revenue — without necessarily improving your return.

**What it is.** When you log into Google Ads and click on a campaign, there is a tab called "Recommendations" and a number at the top of the account called your "Optimization Score," displayed as a percentage from 0% to 100%. Google's algorithm analyses your account — budgets, bids, targeting, ad quality, search term coverage — and generates a list of suggested changes. Each suggestion carries a score impact: if your account shows 72% and one recommendation says "+3.2 pts," implementing it would raise the score to roughly 75%.

The score looks like a grade. It is not. It is a measure of how closely your account follows Google's preferred configuration, which is not the same thing as the most profitable configuration for you. A 100% score means you accepted every suggestion Google made — including raising budgets, broadening match types, and turning on features that give Google more control over your money. An account run profitably at tight budgets will often sit at 60–75% because the owner correctly rejected the "increase your budget by 30%" suggestions. The number is marketing, not a quality metric.

**The four types of recommendation.** They fall into roughly four categories, and the skill is telling them apart:

First, **genuinely helpful**: remove redundant keywords, fix disapproved ads, add missing sitelinks or image assets, enable conversion tracking you missed. These cost nothing and improve quality. Accept them.

Second, **neutral but pushy**: "Upgrade to broad match" gives Google wider discretion over which queries trigger your ads — sometimes that finds new converting terms, sometimes it burns money on irrelevant ones. "Use optimised targeting" lets Google expand your audience beyond what you defined. These are judgment calls that depend on whether your campaign is already profitable at its current settings.

Third, **self-serving**: "Raise your budget to capture X% more impressions." This is the most common recommendation and the most dangerous to accept blindly. Google is telling you that your campaign ran out of daily budget before the day ended, so some auctions were missed. True. What it does not say is whether those missed auctions would have converted at a profitable ROAS. The Efficiency Curve lesson (yesterday) showed that each additional pound of spend buys a progressively worse customer. "Capturing more impressions" often means buying the tail of diminishing returns. Google is saying "you could spend more," not "you should spend more."

Fourth, **auto-applied**: the one that catches owners off guard. Google has a feature called "auto-applied recommendations" where certain changes are made to your account automatically unless you opt out. These can include bid adjustments, keyword additions, and ad text changes. You find and manage this under **Tools & Settings → Auto-applied recommendations**. If a setting changed without anyone touching it, and the Change History shows the actor as "Google (auto-applied)," this is why.

**Why this matters for PMax specifically.** Performance Max campaigns give you fewer manual controls than standard Shopping or Search campaigns — no keywords, no manual bids, limited audience levers. The Recommendations Tab for a PMax campaign therefore focuses heavily on budget suggestions, asset group improvements (more images, videos, headlines), and audience signals. The budget recommendations are where the trap sits. Google will tell you PMAX is "limited by budget" even when more spend would produce diminishing returns. The correct check is not Google's recommendation — it is your own Efficiency Curve from Shopify: what ROAS did you get at this spend level, and does spending more produce proportional returns?

**Worked example.** PMAX CSS AUG spent **£384.02** yesterday — day 6 of its learning phase, 37% above the £280/day recommendation. CPC improved to **£0.70** (best of the phase). If you opened the Recommendations Tab right now, Google would almost certainly not recommend lowering the budget. It would suggest raising it further, or adding more creative assets, or broadening audience signals. Your Optimization Score would rise if you accepted. But the store's ROAS at £384 PMAX + £77 CSS + £222 Facebook = £683 total was **2.38x** — red. The Recommendations Tab cannot tell you this. Only the Shopify-side calculation — £1,629 total sales ÷ £683 total spend = 2.38x — gives you the real answer.

The practical sequence when you take over: (1) open the Recommendations Tab, (2) read what Google suggests, (3) accept the genuinely helpful ones (fix disapprovals, add missing assets), (4) **dismiss every budget-increase suggestion** unless your own Efficiency Curve analysis says the extra spend will pay for itself at 3x ROAS, (5) go to Tools & Settings → Auto-applied recommendations and **turn off every category you don't want Google changing without your knowledge**.

A good answer is a screenshot of the settings page showing each category toggled on or off. An evasive answer is "it's all fine" or "I manage that" without showing you the screen. If budget recommendations are auto-applied, that could explain why PMAX CSS AUG is consistently spending above the daily target — Google may be raising the budget itself.

**You should now be able to:** open the Google Ads Recommendations Tab, distinguish between genuinely helpful suggestions and self-serving budget pushes, check whether auto-applied recommendations are active on the account, and dismiss a "raise your budget" recommendation with confidence when your own ROAS data says the current spend level is already past the efficient frontier.

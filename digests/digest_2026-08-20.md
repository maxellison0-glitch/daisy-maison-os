## 📚 Lesson of the Day

### Traffic Quality — How to Tell Whether Your Ad Spend Is Buying Shoppers or Just Clicks

**Callback:** Yesterday's lesson broke the conversion funnel into four diagnostic steps — session, cart add, checkout, purchase — and showed that the 18 Aug funnel breakdown was a leak downstream of the ad click. Today steps back upstream and asks a harder question: were the people entering that funnel ever going to buy in the first place?

**What it is.** Traffic quality is the proportion of visitors your ad spend delivers who are genuinely considering buying a personalised gift at £15–30, versus the proportion who are window-shoppers, accidental taps, or people looking for something cheaper. The ad platforms do not distinguish. They charge the same CPC whether the person behind the click was ready to personalise a Mr & Mrs sign or was scrolling Instagram on the bus and tapped by mistake. A CPC of £0.30 looks cheap. A CPC of £0.30 that sends 651 people who do not buy is £192.28 of waste.

**How it works.** Facebook and Google both optimise toward the objective you set. If a campaign's optimisation event is set to "link clicks" (or "landing page views"), the algorithm does exactly what you asked: it finds people who are likely to click. Not people likely to buy. The cheapest clickers in any audience pool are the least engaged — they click everything, land on the page, and bounce. This is the cheap click trap, and it is the single most common way small-store Facebook budgets are wasted.

The mechanism is straightforward. Facebook has billions of user profiles, each scored on hundreds of behavioural dimensions. When you tell it to optimise for clicks, it ranks the entire audience pool by click probability and serves the ad to the cheapest-to-click users first. Those are people who click a lot of things — casual browsers, serial ad-tappers, people in Audience Network games. They are cheap because everyone else's campaigns have already learned they do not convert, so the auction price for reaching them is low. Your CPC looks efficient. Your CVR looks broken. But CVR is not broken — you paid to fill your store with the wrong crowd.

**The diagnostics for traffic quality are not CPC.** CPC tells you how much you paid to get someone in the door. The real diagnostics are:

1. **Cart-add rate by source.** If social traffic has a materially lower cart-add rate than search traffic on the same day, the social visitors are lower intent. Shopify does not split cart-add rate by source in standard analytics (you need UTMs or Google Analytics for that), but the pattern is visible in aggregate: when social share rises and CVR falls, the traffic mix is the variable.

2. **CVR trend versus traffic source share.** Look at the last three days: 17 Aug had CVR 5.52% with social at 37.6%. 18 Aug had CVR 2.44% with social at 43.7%. 19 Aug had CVR 2.62% with social at 41.1%. The two sub-3% CVR days are the two highest-social-share days. That correlation does not prove social traffic is junk — but it makes it the first place to investigate.

3. **Bounce rate by campaign.** This requires Google Analytics or the Meta Events Manager, not Shopify. If a campaign's visitors leave without viewing a second page at rates above 70–80%, the traffic is junk. This is the single diagnostic Daryl should be pulling right now.

**Worked example with yesterday's numbers.**

Total spend: £753.29. Total orders: 52. Blended CPA: £753.29 ÷ 52 = **£14.49 per order**.

Now break it by platform. Google Ads (£397.79) targets search intent — people who typed "personalised wedding gift" into Google. Facebook (£355.50) targets discovery — people scrolling who might be interested. If Google's 474 clicks converted at the store's search-sourced CVR of roughly 3–4% (historic range), that is approximately 14–19 orders from Google. That leaves 33–38 orders from all other sources combined (Facebook, direct, returning visitors). Facebook delivered 1,153 clicks (651 + 176 + 326). If those 1,153 clicks produced only 15–20 orders, Facebook's implicit CVR is approximately 1.3–1.7% — half the store average.

£355.50 spent on Facebook for perhaps 15–20 orders gives a CPA of £17.78–£23.70 per order. Against an AOV of £28.96 and roughly 60% gross margin, gross profit per order is about £17.38 — barely covering the ad cost at the top of the CPA range. Facebook may be running at or near breakeven. TOF-WEDDING at £192.28 for 651 clicks is the prime suspect for low-quality traffic at volume.

**The failure mode from the outside** looks like this: CPC is stable or falling (good sign), click volume is high (good sign), but orders stagnate and CVR slides. The natural reaction is "the site is broken" or "just a soft day." The actual cause is that you are buying more clicks from the bottom of the quality barrel. The platform is giving you exactly what you asked for — clicks — and charging less per click because those clicks are worth less.

**The fix** is to check the optimisation event on TOF-WEDDING. If it is set to "link clicks" or "landing page views" rather than "Purchase," the algorithm is intentionally filling the store with browsers. Switching the optimisation event to "Purchase" costs more per click but improves the quality of the person behind it dramatically. You get fewer clicks at a higher CPC — but those clicks buy. TikTok has the same dynamic: when you launch there, set the pixel event to "Complete Payment," never to "View Content" or "Add to Cart."

**ASK DARYL THIS:** "What is the optimisation event on TOF-WEDDING — is it set to link clicks or purchase conversions? Can you show me the Conversion Event setting in the ad set?" A good answer is a screenshot of the ad set settings showing "Purchase" as the conversion event. An evasive answer is "it is set up for the best results" without naming the specific event — that almost always means it is on link clicks or landing page views, which is why the CPC is so cheap.

**You should now be able to:** look at a campaign's CPC and CVR together and judge whether cheap clicks are actually cheap, or whether the low CPC is a symptom of low-quality traffic that will never convert.

## 10. 📚 Lesson of the Day

### The Conversion Funnel as a Diagnostic System

**Builds on:** In the checkout abandonment lesson (17 Jun), we looked at what happens at the payment step — why people who reach the checkout leave before paying. Today we pull back and examine the entire path a visitor takes, from landing on the site to completing a purchase. The checkout step is one station; the funnel has four, and each one isolates a different kind of problem.

**What it is:** The conversion funnel is the sequence of steps every visitor must pass through to become a buyer. Think of it as four doors in a corridor — a visitor enters the first door (the site), and each door they pass through narrows the crowd. The people who walk through all four are your customers. What matters for diagnosis is not just how many make it through — it is *which door* they stop at.

The four doors:

1. **Session** — the visitor lands on the site. This is the top of the funnel. Every paid click, every organic search result, every Instagram tap starts here. The number of sessions is the raw material your ad spend buys.

2. **Cart addition** — the visitor puts something in the cart. This is the first buying signal: "I want this thing enough to take an action." The **cart-add rate** = cart additions ÷ sessions. If this rate is low (say, under 8%), the visitors are landing but not finding anything worth buying. Two possible causes: (a) the ad campaign is sending the wrong people (a Facebook campaign targeting "wedding gifts" that actually delivers to people interested in party supplies), or (b) the landing page is not convincing (poor photos, missing reviews, unclear personalisation options).

3. **Checkout reached** — the visitor enters the checkout flow, fills in their address, sees the shipping options. This is the second commitment: "I am ready to pay." The **cart-to-checkout rate** = checkouts reached ÷ cart additions. If this rate drops below about 50%, something is blocking people between wanting the product and committing to buy. Common causes: surprise costs shown in the cart (tax, shipping estimate), complex cart page, forced account creation, or a slow-loading checkout redirect. The thing to note is that these causes are *site problems*, not *ad problems* — the visitor was interested enough to add to cart, so the ad did its job.

4. **Checkout completed** — the visitor pays. This is the sale. The **checkout completion rate** = completed ÷ reached. If this rate is low (below 60%), people who were ready to pay backed out. This is the "delivery anxiety" layer: they saw the shipping price, the delivery timeframe, or the final total and decided it was not worth it. It can also be a missing payment method (no PayPal, no Apple Pay) or a trust issue (no security badges, unfamiliar checkout).

**Why this matters at the keyboard:** When you see a bad CVR day, the instinct is to blame the ads. But the funnel breakdown tells you whether the ads are actually the problem. If the cart-add rate is fine and the leak is at checkout completion, cutting ad spend is the wrong fix — you would be punishing the ad campaigns for a site problem. Conversely, if the cart-add rate craters while checkout rates stay normal, the ads are sending the wrong traffic and the fix is in the campaigns, not the checkout.

**The three failure shapes you will actually see:**

- **Traffic quality collapse:** Cart-add rate craters (visitors are not interested), but checkout rates stay normal for anyone who does add. This is a campaign problem — cheap clicks from an audience that will never buy.

- **Site friction spike:** Cart-add rate is normal (visitors find products they like), but cart-to-checkout rate drops. Something on the site is blocking them. Could be a bug, a new pop-up, a changed layout.

- **Payment/shipping friction:** Cart-add and cart-to-checkout rates are normal, but checkout completion craters. This is what the Express Teacher Gift Dispatch tier was designed to fix.

When all three rates drop together, the most likely cause is a fundamental traffic quality problem — the campaigns scaled into a low-intent audience that did not engage at any step.

**Worked example — yesterday, 18 August:**

| Step | Count | Rate | Calculation |
|------|-------|------|-------------|
| Sessions | 1,149 | — | — |
| Cart additions | 125 | 10.9% | 125 ÷ 1,149 |
| Reached checkout | 63 | 50.4% | 63 ÷ 125 |
| Completed checkout | 28 | 44.4% | 28 ÷ 63 |
| **CVR** | **2.44%** | | 28 ÷ 1,149 |

Cart-add rate at 10.9% is within the normal 8–12% band — visitors found products they wanted. The leak starts at the second door: only 50.4% of cart-adders reached checkout (down from the typical 60–70% range), and only 44.4% of those who reached checkout completed it (checkout abandonment 55.6%, vs the 28–35% range of the last two weeks). The combination says: visitors engaged with the products, but something downstream — price, shipping, or commitment — pushed them out before paying.

Now apply the arithmetic: if yesterday's CVR had held at 17 Aug's 5.52%, the same 1,149 sessions would have produced ~63 checkout completions instead of 28. At the same AOV of £29.57, that is ~£1,862 total sales instead of £1,537 — and ROAS would have been 2.39x instead of 1.97x. Still red, but 13+ lost orders were lost to funnel friction, not to missing demand.

The operational conclusion: the ads brought 1,149 sessions (healthy), visitors added to cart at a normal rate (10.9%), but the checkout bled them out. The fix is not primarily in the campaigns — it is in whatever happened at the cart-to-checkout and checkout-to-payment transitions. The secondary diagnosis is that TOF-WEDDING's 765 clicks at £0.27 may be bringing lower-intent social traffic that inflates cart adds but drops at the commitment step. A placement breakdown for TOF-WEDDING would confirm or rule this out.

TikTok traffic typically shows even lower checkout completion rates than Facebook because the audience skews younger and more impulsive — high add-to-cart rates, very low follow-through. When TikTok arrives, the funnel diagnostic becomes even more important.

## 📚 Lesson of the Day

### Contribution Margin Per Order — The Number That Tells You Whether Each Sale Is Worth Having

**Callback:** On 13 Jun you learned that ROAS alone doesn't tell you if you're profitable — break-even ROAS is 1 ÷ gross margin, so at 60% margins you need 1.67x just to cover product costs (lesson: ROAS vs profit margin). On 21 Jul you saw the aggregate picture: total sales minus ad spend equals the pool available for everything else (lesson: contribution after ad spend). Today's lesson takes the next step: calculating the actual profit or loss on a single order, because a healthy average can hide orders that lose you money.

### What it is

Contribution margin per order is the money left from one sale after you subtract the costs directly tied to that sale — the product, the packaging, the postage, and the share of ad spend it took to bring that customer in. It is not your full net profit (that would include rent, Shopify subscriptions, labour, and everything else), but it is the profit each order contributes toward covering those fixed costs. If the number is positive, each order helps. If it is negative, each order costs you money no matter how many you sell — and scaling only makes it worse.

The formula:

**Contribution margin = Revenue per order − Product cost − Packaging & postage − Ad cost per order**

The first three are about the product. The last one connects your marketing spend to the individual customer. Each term is something you can either measure or estimate, and together they give you the one number that ROAS alone cannot: whether the business earns money at the order level.

### Ad cost per order

This is the critical bridge between the ad account and the P&L. You calculate it simply: **total ad spend ÷ total Shopify orders**. Not platform-attributed orders — Shopify orders, because that is the real result.

Yesterday: £654.17 ÷ 64 = **£10.22 per order**.

That means every customer who checked out cost £10.22 in advertising to acquire, regardless of which platform, campaign, or click brought them. Some customers came from organic search and cost nothing in ad spend. Some came from a single £0.30 Facebook click. Some came from a £0.71 Google click that needed three visits before converting. The blended number averages all of that, and it is the figure that actually left the bank account.

### Working it through with yesterday's numbers

| Component | Value |
|-----------|-------|
| Average order value (£1,810 total ÷ 64 orders) | £28.28 |
| Estimated product cost (~12% of gross price) | ~£3.40 |
| Packaging & postage (estimated) | ~£4.50 |
| Ad cost per order (£654 ÷ 64) | £10.22 |
| **Contribution margin per order** | **~£10.16** |

That is roughly £10 per order flowing toward fixed costs and profit. On 64 orders, that is about **£650 in total contribution for the day** — which, by coincidence, is almost exactly yesterday's ad spend. In practical terms: the advertising paid for itself, the product and postage costs were covered, and the remaining revenue is available for overheads and profit. You are not getting rich at 2.77x, but you are not losing money either.

### Where the upsells fit — and why they matter more than you think

Yesterday's upsells (Medium £95.84, Large £107.88, Easel £53.55, Gift Wrap £23.80, Strips £17.91, Shipping Protection £3.98) added **£302.96** to gross sales. These cost almost nothing to supply: a mounting strip costs pennies, a size upgrade is a toggle in the order system, an easel is a few pounds wholesale. Call the variable cost £30 across all of them — generous. That leaves **~£273 of near-pure profit** spread across 64 orders = **£4.27 per order**.

Without upsells, the contribution margin drops from ~£10.16 to roughly **£5.89**. The upsell programme added 72% to the per-order profit.

This is why you track upsell revenue as a percentage of gross every single day. It is not a "nice to have" line item buried in the digest. It is the difference between a £10 contribution and a £6 contribution. At 64 orders per day, that difference is £256. Over a month, that is roughly **£7,700** — more than seven times what you pay Daryl.

### Why this changes how you think about ROAS

A 2.77x ROAS looks the same on the dashboard whether the store has upsells or not. But without upsells, the contribution margin drops by almost half, and the business needs many more orders to cover its fixed costs. Two Shopify stores can both run at 2.77x ROAS and one can be thriving while the other is barely surviving. The difference is what happens between the click and the bank account: the product margin, the upsell stack, the postage cost, the packaging.

ROAS is the headline number. Contribution margin per order is the number that tells you whether the headline is good news or bad news.

### ASK DARYL THIS

"What's our cost per acquisition running at this week across the whole account?" (It is total spend ÷ total purchase conversions in Ads Manager, not per-campaign.) A good answer gives you a specific number and compares it to AOV. An evasive answer talks about ROAS without ever mentioning the actual cost per customer. If his CPA figure doesn't roughly match your Shopify-derived £10.22, ask why — the gap is the attribution disagreement, and understanding where platform-reported conversions differ from Shopify orders is worth a five-minute conversation.

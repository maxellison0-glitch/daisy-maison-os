## 📚 Lesson of the Day (confidential — not for Daryl)

### Google's Daily Budget Overspend Rule — Why "GBP 300/day" Doesn't Mean GBP 300/day

**Callback:** The budget pacing lesson from 15 Jul explained how platforms don't spend evenly through the day — they front-load spend on high-value hours and slow down when auctions are thin. Today's lesson takes the next step: how Google manages the DAILY total over the course of a month, and why "set the budget to GBP 300" can still produce a GBP 375 or even a GBP 600 day.

When you set a **daily budget** in Google Ads — say GBP 300 for the PMAX CSS DEC campaign — you are telling Google: "I'm willing to spend around GBP 300 per day, and my monthly ceiling is GBP 300 × 30.4 = GBP 9,120 for this campaign." The 30.4 multiplier is Google's standard approximation of the average number of days in a month. That **monthly ceiling** is the real constraint Google optimises against, not the daily number. The daily budget is a guideline that Google treats as an average, not a hard cap.

A **daily budget** is the number you type into the campaign settings in Google Ads. A **monthly spending limit** is the daily budget multiplied by 30.4, and is the true ceiling Google enforces. Google will never — in theory — let a campaign exceed its monthly limit over a full calendar month, though it may overshoot on individual days to compensate with underspend on others.

Here is the mechanical rule: Google's Smart Bidding algorithms look at each day's available auctions and decide where to bid. On days when there are lots of high-quality auctions available — a busy Thursday when many people are searching for wedding gifts, for instance — Google will spend MORE than the daily budget because the algorithm calculates that overshooting today and underspending on a quieter day still keeps the monthly total within the ceiling. Google calls this **overdelivery** and explicitly allows itself to spend **up to 2× the daily budget on any single day**. So a GBP 300/day budget can produce any daily spend from GBP 0 to GBP 600. This is not a bug or a sign of a runaway campaign — it is documented behaviour that Google considers a feature.

The important consequence for managing the account: when someone says "cut the budget to GBP 300/day," there are actually two questions to answer. First, what daily budget figure is set in the Google Ads interface? Second, what did Google actually deliver against that figure? If the daily budget IS set to GBP 300 and Google delivered GBP 375, that's within the 2× rule and is expected behaviour — Google thought Thursday's wedding-gift auctions were worth overshooting for. But if the daily budget is set to GBP 380 and Google delivered GBP 375, there was no overspend at all — the budget was never actually cut to GBP 300 in the first place. These two scenarios look identical from the outside (both show GBP 375 spend) but have completely different fixes.

If the budget IS set to GBP 300 and Google keeps overshooting: two levers. First, lower the daily budget further — setting it to GBP 250 means even at maximum 2× overspend, Google cannot exceed GBP 500, and the monthly cap drops to GBP 7,600 which forces discipline. Second, tighten the **target ROAS** (tROAS) — this is a separate setting that tells Google's algorithm how selective to be about WHICH auctions to enter. A higher tROAS target (say 3.5×) makes Google skip low-quality auctions entirely, reducing spend naturally without lowering the budget. The combination of both levers — a lower budget AND a tighter tROAS — is the strongest form of constraint. If the budget was never set to GBP 300 at all: the fix is much simpler — apply the change.

**Worked example using yesterday's real numbers.** PMAX CSS DEC spent GBP 374.95 on 30 Jul. The digest called for GBP 300–310/day from 26 Jul. Over the full month of July (30 days), PMAX CSS DEC has spent a total of GBP 9,146.42 — that's an average of **GBP 304.88/day**, which actually lands inside the GBP 300–310 target when viewed as a monthly average. If the daily budget is set to GBP 300, the monthly cap is GBP 300 × 30.4 = GBP 9,120, and the campaign has already slightly exceeded that with one day remaining. But the recent five-day run tells a different story: GBP 365.84, GBP 307.85, GBP 422.85, GBP 362.36, GBP 374.95 — an average of GBP 366.77/day, well above the target. This pattern — the monthly average looking on-target while recent days consistently overshoot — is exactly what happens when a budget was lower earlier in the month (averaging GBP 260–280 in early July when teacher spend took priority) and Google is now "catching up" by spending more in late July because it has headroom under the monthly cap from earlier underspend.

The arithmetic: if Google spent ~GBP 270/day for the first 20 days (GBP 5,400) and then ramped to ~GBP 375/day for the last 10 days (GBP 3,750), the month total is GBP 9,150 — almost exactly matching the GBP 9,146.42 actual. Google sees the monthly cap as the real constraint, so it uses late-month headroom to enter more auctions. This is not Daryl gaming the budget; it is the algorithm doing exactly what it was designed to do.

**ASK DARYL THIS:** "What is the actual daily budget currently set for PMAX CSS DEC in the Google Ads interface — the exact number, not what we agreed to verbally?" A good answer is a specific figure, ideally with a screenshot or a shared screen showing the campaign settings. An evasive answer is "I've adjusted it" or "it's roughly GBP 300" without showing the interface — because "roughly GBP 300" could mean GBP 350 in the settings, which allows Google to spend up to GBP 700 on a single day and GBP 10,640 in the month. The number in the interface is the only number that matters.

**You should now be able to:** distinguish between Google overshooting a correctly-set daily budget (expected behaviour — fix with a lower budget or tighter tROAS) and a budget that was never actually set to the number agreed upon — and know that the specific interface figure, not a verbal confirmation, is the only way to tell which.

## 📚 Lesson of the Day

### Cohort Repeat Rate — How to Measure Whether Customers Actually Come Back

**Callback:** In the CLV lesson (22 Jun), you learned that a customer's first purchase is not the end of the story — their future purchases change the break-even CPA ceiling. That was the theory. Today you learn how to measure the reality: how many customers actually come back, how long it takes, and what this specific store's repeat pattern means for ad spend decisions.

**What it is.** A cohort is a group of customers who all made their first purchase in the same period — say, everyone who bought for the first time in July 2026. The cohort repeat rate is the percentage of that group who come back and buy again within a defined window. Shopify reports this in two places: the "Returning customer rate" in Analytics (the percentage of yesterday's orders that came from someone who has bought before), and the deeper "Returning customers over time" report (which shows the trend month-on-month). The headline number on the Analytics dashboard is a snapshot — "X% of today's orders were repeat buyers." The trend report is what matters.

**Why it matters for ad spend.** Every returning customer who buys again did so at zero acquisition cost — they arrived via Direct, a bookmark, an old email, or dark social. They cost nothing in ad spend. That means your blended ROAS — the number this digest reports every morning — is always higher than your true new-customer ROAS, because it mixes free repeat revenue with paid-for new revenue in the same total.

Here is the mechanism. Suppose your returning customer rate is 15% (a reasonable estimate for a personalised gift store — higher than luxury furniture, lower than coffee subscriptions). Yesterday's 47 orders would split roughly 40 new customers and 7 repeat buyers. Those 7 repeat orders generated approximately £200 in revenue at zero ad cost. Strip them out: the remaining 40 new-customer orders earned £1,142 on £517 of ad spend = **2.21x new-customer ROAS**. The blended 2.60x includes free money. The 2.21x is what your ads actually achieved.

This matters because the ad budget decision should be governed by marginal new-customer ROAS, not blended ROAS. If you scale spend by 20%, you are buying more new customers only — the repeat layer does not grow proportionally. So the marginal return on that extra spend is closer to 2.21x than 2.60x, and you need to know that before deciding to scale.

**The personalised-gift wrinkle.** In a consumable business — coffee, skincare, supplements — the repeat cycle is 30–60 days. You can measure cohort performance within a quarter. In personalised gifts, the cycle is 6–12 months and occasion-driven: the couple who bought a Mr & Mrs sign in August might buy a christening pebble in March, a family sign at Christmas, and a teacher gift next July. Your repeat rate looks low at 30 days and only reveals itself at 12 months. This means two things: (a) you need to look at 12-month cohort retention, not 30-day, and (b) your email list and remarketing audiences are the mechanism that makes the repeat happen — without a prompt (a Christmas email, a Mother's Day reminder), the repeat purchase does not self-trigger the way a coffee reorder does.

**How to find it.** In Shopify: Analytics → Reports → search "Returning customers over time." Set the date range to the last 12 months. The report shows two lines: new customers and returning customers per day/week/month. What you want to see is the returning-customer line growing over time — that means the buyer pool is compounding. If it is flat while your ad spend rises, you are buying one-time customers and the CLV assumption is a fiction.

The second diagnostic is the "First-time vs returning customer sales" report, which shows revenue split. If returning customers contribute 10% of revenue and you are spending 50% of revenue on ads, you are heavily dependent on paid acquisition. If returning customers contribute 25%, the business has a cushion — a bad ad week hurts less because a quarter of revenue arrives for free.

**Worked example with yesterday's numbers.** Total revenue £1,342 on £517 spend = 2.60x ROAS. At an estimated 15% returning-customer rate: £201 of that revenue is repeat (zero cost), £1,141 is new-customer revenue. New-customer ROAS = £1,141 / £517 = 2.21x. Break-even ROAS at ~40% gross margin is ~1.67x (from the 13 Jun lesson). So the new-customer layer is still profitable at 2.21x (£1 of spend generates £2.21 of new revenue, of which £0.88 is gross profit, minus the £1 spent = £0.12/order net before overhead). Thin — but not negative. And the 15% repeat rate adds ~£0.30 of free gross profit per original customer over the next 12 months. That is the difference between a viable business and a treadmill.

## 📚 Lesson of the Day

### Facebook Bidding Strategies: Highest Volume, Cost Cap, and Bid Cap

**Building on:** In June's Smart Bidding lesson (14 Jun), we covered how automated bid strategies work in principle — the algorithm adjusts bids auction-by-auction using signals like device, time, and audience. Today goes inside Facebook specifically, because something changed in the account yesterday that makes this immediately practical.

---

Every time someone scrolls their Facebook or Instagram feed, an auction runs in milliseconds. Facebook decides: should this person see your ad, someone else's, or no ad at all? Your campaign's **bidding strategy** is the instruction you give Facebook about how hard to compete in those auctions. There are three main options, and each one gives Facebook a different amount of freedom with your money.

**Highest Volume** (the default — Facebook sometimes calls it "Lowest Cost") is the simplest. You set a daily budget — say £150 — and tell Facebook: "Spend it all. Get me as many results as possible." Facebook then bids whatever it needs to win auctions, limited only by your daily budget ceiling. The upside is obvious: you will almost always spend your full budget, and the algorithm finds the cheapest results it can. The downside is less obvious but much more important: **Facebook does not care about your cost per result.** It finds the cheapest conversions first — the people who were already going to buy — and then works its way outward to more expensive, less interested people to use up the remaining budget. Early in the day, clicks might cost £0.20. By evening, it is paying £0.35 for someone half as likely to buy. On a small budget this does not matter much. On a large one, the last 30% of spend can produce almost nothing.

**Cost Cap** adds a guardrail. You still set a daily budget, but you also set a target cost per result — for example, "I want conversions at no more than £10 each on average." Facebook will spend money as long as it can find people within that target. When it runs out of cheap enough prospects, it stops spending. The advantage is protection: your unit economics stay in line. The risk — and this catches people — is that Facebook might not spend your full budget. You set £200/day, but the algorithm only finds £80 worth of conversions at your target price, so it spends £80 and sits idle. This looks like the campaign is "broken" or "not delivering," but it is actually the algorithm protecting you from overpriced traffic. Understanding this distinction is the difference between panicking at a low-spend day and recognising good discipline.

**Bid Cap** is the strictest control. You set the absolute maximum Facebook can bid in any single auction — not an average like Cost Cap, but a hard ceiling on every individual bid. If you set a £0.50 bid cap, Facebook will never pay more than £0.50 per click in any auction, period. The advantage is maximum control. The downside: if your cap is too aggressive, you lose most auctions and barely spend anything. Bid Cap is a precision tool for experienced advertisers; most Shopify store campaigns run on Highest Volume.

---

**Why this matters for daily spend swings:**

When a campaign is set to **Highest Volume**, Facebook can legally spend up to **75% MORE** than your daily budget on any given day, as long as it averages out over the billing week. (Meta changed this from 25% to 75% in recent years — it is a bigger lever than most people realise.) So a campaign with a £150/day budget could spend **£262** on a Monday if Facebook finds good opportunities, then only **£38** on a quieter day to balance the weekly average.

This is why you can set a budget and then see wildly different daily spend without anyone touching the controls. The question every morning is: **did the spend jump because (a) the algorithm found more opportunity at the current budget, or (b) someone changed the budget?** Only (b) is a human decision. (a) is the algorithm doing its job within the rules you gave it.

**What the failure looks like from the outside:** The most common trap is Highest Volume with a budget increase. You raise the budget from £125 to £225, and spend jumps. But each additional pound buys a slightly less interested person than the one before, because the algorithm already found the best prospects first. Revenue does not scale proportionally. ROAS drops. You conclude "the account is broken" when actually the account is doing exactly what Highest Volume does at scale: spending every penny, regardless of quality.

---

**Worked example with yesterday's Daisy numbers:**

TOF-WEDDING spent **£225.27** yesterday. On 3 Aug, it spent **£123.01**. That is an **83% increase**. The 75% daily overspend allowance from a £123 average would cap a single day at £123 × 1.75 = **£215**. Yesterday's £225 exceeds that, which means either:

(a) **Daryl raised the daily budget** from ~£125 to ~£225 or higher, OR
(b) **The daily budget was already set higher** (say £200+) and the 3 Aug spend of £123 was an *underspend* day, with yesterday's £225 falling within the 75% overspend from a £200 base (£200 × 1.75 = £350, so £225 fits).

From Shopify and Windsor data alone, we cannot tell which. The answer is inside Facebook Ads Manager. What we *can* see is the result:

- CPC stayed cheap at **£0.26** (vs £0.25 on 3 Aug) — traffic was not more expensive per click.
- **866 clicks** (vs 492 on 3 Aug) — nearly double the volume.
- Combined ROAS dropped from **3.21x → 2.37x**.
- The extra ~374 clicks did **not** produce proportionally more Shopify orders.

The arithmetic: £813.59 total spend × 3.21x (3 Aug's ROAS) would have required **£2,612** total sales to stay green. Actual was £1,931. The missing **£681** is the revenue gap that the extra Facebook spend failed to fill. The CPC says the traffic is cheap. The ROAS says it is not converting. That is the signature of a Top-of-Funnel campaign that has scaled past its efficient ceiling — exactly the diminishing returns lesson from 28 Jul, now playing out on the Facebook side instead of Google.

---

**Ask Daryl this:**

"What bidding strategy is TOF-WEDDING set to — Highest Volume, Cost Cap, or Bid Cap? And what is the current daily budget? The spend jumped from £123 on 3 Aug to £225 yesterday. Did you raise the budget, or did Facebook auto-pace up?"

A good answer sounds like: *"I raised it to £X on [date] because [reason]"* — clear, specific, a deliberate decision with a rationale. Or: *"The budget is £200, it paced high because Monday had good inventory"* — also fine, shows awareness.

An evasive answer sounds like: *"I let the algorithm optimise"* without naming the specific budget or strategy. That tells you either he does not know the bidding strategy that is set — which means the campaign is on autopilot — or he does not want to explain the budget change. Either answer tells you something useful about how actively the account is being managed.

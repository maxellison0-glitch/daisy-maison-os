## Lesson of the Day

### Minimum ROAS Bidding on Facebook (the ROAS Goal Strategy)

**Callback:** The 12 Aug lesson covered three Facebook bidding strategies — Highest Volume (spend freely, find the cheapest actions), Cost Cap (stay under a target cost per action), and Bid Cap (hard ceiling on each auction bid). Min ROAS is the fourth strategy, and as of yesterday it appeared in your account for the first time on the new CHRISTMAS STATIC POSTS campaign.

**What it is, in plain English**

Minimum ROAS is Facebook's way of letting you say: "Only spend my money when you expect the return on that spend to clear a floor I set." Internally, Facebook calls it `LOWEST_COST_WITH_MIN_ROAS` — that name reveals the mechanism. It works exactly like Lowest Cost (the strategy on every other campaign in the account), with one added constraint: before entering each auction, the algorithm checks whether the expected revenue from converting that person, divided by what it would cost to show them the ad, exceeds the ROAS floor you set. If it does, it bids. If it doesn't, it walks away.

The word "minimum" is doing heavy lifting. It is not a target — it is a floor. Facebook will still try to maximise total conversion value above that floor, but it will refuse to spend a single penny on any auction where the expected return falls short. The practical effect: delivery throttles itself. On a day when the available audience is high-intent (wedding season peak, payday weekends), the campaign spends up to budget. On a day when the only remaining people are low-probability converters, the campaign spends almost nothing — and that is by design, not a failure.

**Why this matters — and how it differs from what you've seen**

Every other campaign in the Daisy account uses Lowest Cost Without Cap (also called Highest Volume). That strategy tells Facebook: "Spend my daily budget and find me the most conversions you can — I don't care what they cost individually." It is the default. It is good for volume. But it is blind to whether any individual conversion is actually profitable.

Cost Cap (from the 12 Aug lesson) adds a constraint on the cost side: "Don't pay more than £X per purchase." It controls cost per event — good when you know your break-even CPA but don't care about the revenue per order.

Min ROAS adds a constraint on the return side: "Don't spend unless the expected revenue from this person, divided by what you're paying to reach them, exceeds Xx." It controls return on spend — the metric that actually tells you whether the store is making money. For a store like Daisy where AOV varies dramatically (a £9 teacher keyring vs a £45 street sign with size upgrade and easel), controlling ROAS is more useful than controlling CPA, because a £15 CPA is fine on a £45 basket but ruinous on a £9 one.

**The failure mode you need to know**

If the ROAS floor is set too high, the campaign will not spend at all. Facebook will enter auctions, calculate the expected return, judge it insufficient, and walk away from every single one. You will see a £10/day budget, £0 spend, and zero impressions. That is not a bug — it is the algorithm doing exactly what you told it to. The fix is to lower the ROAS floor, not to raise the budget. The budget is only the ceiling; the ROAS floor is the actual throttle.

If the ROAS floor is set too low, the campaign behaves identically to Lowest Cost — the constraint never binds, so it is as if it does not exist. You are paying for the feature without getting the benefit.

The sweet spot is a ROAS floor at roughly 60–80% of your actual target. If you want 3.0x ROAS from the campaign, set the floor at 2.0x–2.5x. Facebook will avoid the obviously bad auctions (the ones that would drag you below 2.0x) while still bidding on marginal ones that might or might not hit 3.0x. You accept some sub-3.0x conversions but avoid the sub-2.0x ones that destroy the average.

**Worked example: CHRISTMAS STATIC POSTS**

Yesterday's new campaign: £10/day budget, Min ROAS bidding, two test ad sets (test_xmas_creatives £4.82, test_xmas_creatives2 £3.08). Combined spend £7.90, 26 clicks, zero purchases.

This is the correct strategy choice for an early seasonal test. Christmas is 112 days away. The audience is not in buying mode yet — someone seeing a Christmas gift ad in early September might click out of curiosity but is unlikely to purchase today. Min ROAS protects against that: if the algorithm judges a click-but-no-buy audience, it throttles spend to near zero instead of burning £10/day on curiosity clicks. The £7.90 actually spent (vs the £10 budget) means the algorithm found some people it believed could convert profitably — it just did not land any purchases on day one, which is expected.

Compare this to what would happen with Lowest Cost at £10/day: the full £10 would be spent regardless, buying 30–35 clicks of pre-Christmas browsers with near-zero conversion probability. Over 90 days until serious Christmas buying begins, that would be ~£900 in curiosity clicks. Min ROAS caps that leak automatically.

The unknown: what ROAS floor did Daryl set? The campaign's ROAS floor value is not visible through Windsor — it is only in Ads Manager (campaign → Edit → Bid Strategy → ROAS Control field).

**ASK DARYL THIS:** "What Minimum ROAS value did you set on the CHRISTMAS STATIC POSTS campaign, and what made you choose that number?" A good answer names a specific multiple (e.g. 2.0x or 4.0x) and explains the reasoning — an aggressive floor (say 2.0x) maximises early reach and data collection, a conservative floor (say 4.0x+) protects spend during a warm-up period but risks starving the algorithm of learning data. An evasive answer is "Facebook handles it" or "it's the default" — that means the floor may be set too low to provide any protection, which defeats the purpose of choosing this strategy over Lowest Cost.

**You should now be able to** look at any Facebook campaign's bid strategy in Windsor, identify whether it uses Min ROAS vs Lowest Cost vs Cost Cap, and understand that low spend on a Min ROAS campaign is the strategy working correctly (not a delivery failure) — while zero spend means the floor is set too aggressively for the current demand pool and needs lowering.
